## 1. TDEE Equations (Deep)

### 1.1 Mifflin-St Jeor — the state of the evidence

Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) predicts RMR from weight, height, age, sex: men `10·W + 6.25·H − 5·A + 5`, women `10·W + 6.25·H − 5·A − 161`, derived from 498 healthy adults (19–78 y, BMI 17–42) (methodology cites: https://tdeecal.com/methodology/, https://tdeecal.com/sources/). It is the equation recommended by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics for weight-management energy needs (https://www.jandonline.org/article/S0002-8223(03)00982-9/abstract).

Accuracy across validation studies (measured via indirect calorimetry, accuracy = % of individuals within ±10% of measured RMR):

- Frankenfield et al. 2005: Mifflin was the most accurate of the four standards tested; >10% error in 22% of subjects (vs 33% Harris-Benedict, 35% Owen) (https://www.andeal.org/files/Docs/Frankenfield_et_al_2005%5B1%5D.pdf).
- Clinical Nutrition 2013 (n=337): Mifflin was the only unbiased equation (95% CI −26 to +8 kcal/d); 82% accuracy overall, 87% non-obese / 75% obese (https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614(13)00100-3/abstract).
- J Nutritional Science 2020 (125 women, BMI 17–44): Mifflin and Henry were the only unbiased equations at group level; Mifflin most accurate at individual level (71% within ±10%, Henry 66%); ~1/3 of individuals still misclassified; accuracy worst at BMI extremes; equation variables explain only ~15% of bias variance (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-nutritional-science/article/validity-of-predictive-equations-to-estimate-rmr-in-females-with-varying-bmi/9EB5881462F01C1082D52F5E78FAF691).
- Frontiers in Endocrinology 2018 (n=4,247 morbidly obese): Mifflin had the lowest bias (−25.8 kcal/d) and best agreement of all equations tested, but accuracy was only ~56% (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/endocrinology/articles/10.3389/fendo.2018.00367/full).
- 2011 cross-validation (MedGem criterion): Harris-Benedict actually performed best in that sample — individual-level results vary by population; no equation is robust for individuals (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1440244011000417).
- Athletes (2023 Sports Medicine meta, 29 studies, 1,430 participants): Mifflin significantly **under**estimated RMR (~5%); only ~50% of athletes within ±10%; the Ten-Haaf equation was most precise (80.2% within ±10%); heterogeneity I² = 80–93% for most equations (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01896-z).

**Bottom line for PersonalOS:** Mifflin is the right default — unbiased at group level and most accurate in the general population including obesity, which is exactly the population a life-management app serves. But individual error is the rule, not the exception: expect ~20–30% of users to be >10% off, i.e., ~±200–400 kcal/d RMR error before any activity multiplier. tdeecalculator.net's own FAQ concedes ~10% error (~230 kcal on a 2,300 kcal/day estimate) (https://tdeecalculator.net/faq.php). MacroFactor states individual errors >500 kcal/d are common and >1,000 kcal/d not unheard of for formula-based TDEE (https://macrofactor.com/algorithm-accuracy/). This is the fundamental argument for adaptive expenditure (section 3).

### 1.2 The activity multiplier (PAL) — the single biggest error source

TDEE = RMR × PAL, where PAL multipliers (1.2 / 1.375 / 1.55 / 1.725 / 1.9) derive from the FAO/WHO/UNU 2004 energy report (https://tdeecalculator.org/methodology/, https://tdeecal.com/methodology/). The multiplier is the dominant error term for five compounding reasons:

1. **Self-report overestimation.** ~80% of users pick one activity level too high, inflating calories by ~250–300 kcal/day (https://tdeecalculator.org/activity-level-guide/). People overreport exercise duration ~51% on average (Dhurandhar 2015, cited at https://fitnessvolt.com/tdee-calculator/accuracy/). "Moderately active" (1.55) requires 3–5 genuinely hard sessions **plus** active non-exercise hours; three lifting sessions on a desk job is "lightly active" at best (https://tdeecalculator.org/activity-level-guide/).
2. **The 1-MET baseline is wrong for individuals.** 1 MET = 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min ≈ 1 kcal/kg/h overestimates true resting metabolic rate by 10–30% (https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/revisiting-the-calculation-of-pal-from-physical-activity-data/) and by 35% (VO₂) / 20% (kcal) in a 769-person sample (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4428382/). Any PAL derived from the MET system inherits this bias; BMI-specific MET correction factors are needed (Wilms et al., cited ibid.).
3. **PAL is a ratio, so errors multiply.** RMR error × PAL error compound; PAL also assumes PAEE scales with the same factors as RMR — a questionable assumption (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-66767-7).
4. **Questionnaire validity is poor at the individual level.** Meta-analysis vs doubly labeled water (DLW): 25 of 46 questionnaires underestimated TEE, 21 overestimated; nearly none had acceptable individual limits of agreement; only 2 met the ±10% / r>0.6 bar (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/comparison-of-total-and-activity-energy-expenditure-estimates-from-physical-activity-questionnaires-and-doubly-labelled-water-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/06F17750B5B738E59A02ACA00414E81A). A 6,497-measurement DLW prediction equation explains only ~69% of TEE variance, absolute error ~11.2% (https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-024-01089-5).
5. **Error is biased by body size.** Higher body-fat people overreport activity more (Bouchard 1999: overestimation grows with %BF; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/j.1550-8528.1999.tb00387.x; Irwin 2001: EE overestimated 16.7% in men ≥50 y and 19.7% at BF ≥30% vs 5.3%/5.6% in younger/leaner; https://doi.org/10.1038/oby.2001.68).

### 1.3 Implications for an auto-recomputed TDEE; validation of B4

- **The multiplier is a static guess; the weight trend is a measurement.** Every error source above is a *fixed* error embedded in a formula TDEE. An adaptive TDEE from weight + intake observes the real outcome and cancels all of it (MacroFactor: after 3–4 weeks of logging, recommendation accuracy is ~120–170% better than formula-based TDEE — typical errors 55–63% smaller; median 100-day expenditure error ≈108 kcal vs formula errors commonly >500 kcal; https://macrofactor.com/algorithm-accuracy/). **PersonalOS's weekly recompute on rolling weight already replaces the multiplier with data** — that is the single biggest accuracy upgrade available, and it costs nothing.
- **B4 ("manual TDEE override freezes all targets") is validated with one caveat.** Rationale: a user who enters a measured/known TDEE is asserting better information than the equation; freezing targets honors that and makes the override explainable (no silent drift). Caveat: a frozen value that is never revisited can become stale (activity changes, metabolic adaptation — see §3.6/§6.4). Recommendation: keep the recompute pipeline running in the background and show "implied TDEE from your data" vs the locked value at weekly check-up, so the freeze is a *conscious* freeze, not a forgotten one. This preserves the letter and spirit of B4 (the user always chooses), while preventing the classic failure of static targets (MacroFactor's key critique of every other app: "most apps don't update targets even when they're demonstrably wrong"; https://macrofactor.com/algorithm-accuracy/).
- Initial-estimate note: MacroFactor seeds with the Cunningham equation (FFM-based) + custom multipliers (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/230-what-is-total-daily-energy-expenditure-tdee, https://help.macrofactorapp.com/dashboard/expenditure). Mifflin needs no body-fat input and is the right offline-first choice; if PersonalOS ever asks for body fat %, an optional Cunningham seed is the documented upgrade path.

## 2. The 7700 kcal/kg Assumption

### 2.1 Origin

Max Wishnofsky (1958) asked "what is the caloric equivalent of one pound of body weight gained or lost?" and answered 3,500 kcal/lb (≡ 7,700 kcal/kg). Derivation: Bozenrad's 1911 chemical analysis — human adipose tissue is ~87% fat; fat at bomb-calorimetry energy density 9.5 kcal/g → ~3,750 kcal/lb of adipose; cross-checked against the 1930s Strang 59-day VLCD studies (−2,100 kcal/d, −0.6 lb/d → 3,500 kcal/lb) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4035446/). Fifty years of textbook/lay reuse ("Wishnofsky's Rule") followed.

### 2.2 Accuracy — and when it breaks

Hall's model-based analyses show the energy density of weight change is **not constant**:

- It depends on initial body fat: the 32.2 MJ/kg (7,700 kcal/kg) rule matches obese subjects with >30 kg initial fat but **overestimates the deficit required per kg for leaner people** (e.g., a 15 kg loss from 20 kg initial fat has energy density ≈24.7 MJ/kg ≈ 5,900 kcal/kg) (Hall 2008, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376744/).
- Weight loss has two phases: an early rapid phase dominated by glycogen, protein, and bound water (low energy density) and a later fat-dominated phase approaching 7,700. In CALERIE, the measured energy content of weight change at week 4 was 4,858 kcal/kg (2,208 kcal/lb) — 37% below the rule (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4035446/).
- Composition follows the Forbes model: the fat-free fraction of weight change is a function of initial fat mass (FFM/fat-mass relation: dFFM/dBW = 10.4/(10.4+FM)); the "quarter FFM rule" (ΔFFM ≈ 0.25·ΔBW) is a rough average, with substantial temporal and individual variation (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/obr.12143; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376748/; model detail: https://www.pbrc.edu/research-and-faculty/calculators/weight-loss-predictor/about/Aboutthemodel.pdf — FFM energy density ~1,020 kcal/kg, fat ~9,500 kcal/kg).

**When 7700 breaks in practice (single-user app context):** (a) the first 1–3 weeks of any deficit/surplus (glycogen + water, sodium shifts); (b) large carb or salt swings (glycogen binds ~3 g water/g glycogen); (c) aggressive rates or extreme body composition; (d) refeed transitions (the bar can show "missing" deficit while the scale obeys water, not fat). Over multi-week trended windows, 7,700 is a sound operational constant: MacroFactor explicitly uses 3,500/lb in its accuracy math and reports median expenditure error ≈108 kcal over 100 days — the constant works when applied to *trended* weight (https://macrofactor.com/algorithm-accuracy/).

### 2.3 How to present it

Estimate framing, per the explainable-math mandate: "7,700 kcal ≈ energy content of **1 kg of fat tissue** (Wishnofsky 1958, from adipose composition); it is the fat-dominated steady-state value. Early weeks and water/glycogen swings can make the scale diverge by 30–40%+; judge the rate over 2+ week trends, never from single weigh-ins." This is exactly how the value should be surfaced in the check-up "show your work" line: rate × 7700/7 is the *fat component* of the target, not a promise about the scale.

## 3. Adaptive Expenditure (Deep — the key section)

### 3.1 The core idea (MacroFactor)

Expenditure is a **prediction engine**, not a formula: start from the energy balance identity `Calories in − Calories out = change in stored energy`, rearrange to `Calories out = Calories in − change in stored energy`, estimate "change in stored energy" from the *trend* in weight (rate × energy content of the weight change, with lean-vs-fat composition assumptions), and solve for expenditure (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/dashboard/expenditure). Worked example from their docs: surplus of +200 kcal/d inferred from trend weight + logged intake of 3,000 kcal/d → expenditure ≈ 2,800 kcal/d (ibid.).

The algorithm is a **predictor-corrector**: it tacitly predicts your weight change from (intake − estimated expenditure); the difference between predicted and observed weight change corrects the expenditure estimate (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/, https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/).

### 3.2 The two inputs and their noise problems

1. **Weight trend.** Scale weight is noisy (water, glycogen, gut contents); the app interpolates missing weigh-ins linearly, then applies a weighted-moving-average-style trend that weights recent data more but still "cares" about a long back-window; trend updates are conservative — a 1-day deviation is ignored, a 4–5-day sustained/accelerating deviation starts moving the trend (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/21-weight-trend, https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/). The change rate used for the energy-balance inference is trend-weight change over the past **20 days**, expressed as a weekly figure (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/19-change-rate).
2. **Intake data.** "Calories in" is logged intake (not prescribed targets — "adherence-neutral" by design: the system solves for expenditure regardless of whether you hit the previous week's program; https://help.macrofactorapp.com/dashboard/expenditure). Data completeness matters: V2 needed ~80–85% nutrition completeness (≥6 of 7 days logged) or it paused updates; V3 can tolerate more missing data by inferring unlogged days' intake within ~15–20% of actual (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/). Its one named Achilles heel: **partial logging** (a half-logged day reads as under-eating and drags expenditure down) (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/dashboard/expenditure).

### 3.3 The math that matters: stability vs responsiveness

"You can't simultaneously maximize stability and responsiveness" — a year-long window is stable but takes months to adapt; a 1-day window adapts instantly but bounces ±500 kcal (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/). MacroFactor's resolution: a long-memory trend with recency weighting (weight trending), plus a two-step response — week 1 hedges, weeks 2–3 commit if the trend holds (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/26-how-should-i-interpret-changes-to-my-energy-expenditure). V3 improved both axes vs V2: day-to-day expenditure changes ~35% smaller, trend reversals detected 1–5 days sooner, ~10% more accurate (weekly prediction errors ~15% smaller) (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/).

**One V3 bug fix every rule-based clone must copy:** V1/V2 assumed the deficit implied by losing X lbs/wk is *larger* than the surplus implied by gaining X lbs/wk (muscle gain composition). Over noisy weeks, that asymmetry created a persistent **~80 kcal upward drift** in estimated expenditure; V3 uses symmetric energy content for gain vs loss (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/). A signed, additive rate model (PersonalOS's `rate × 7700/7`) is symmetric by construction — it does not have this bug.

### 3.4 Guardrails (recreatable rule-based)

- **Pause, don't guess:** insufficient data → hold updates (MacroFactor pauses expenditure when nutrition completeness <6/7 days, or when weight logging stops; one missing weight entry pauses, one new entry resumes) (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/, https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates).
- **Conservative first response, committed second:** hedge week 1, larger adjustment only if the trend holds (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/26-how-should-i-interpret-changes-to-my-energy-expenditure).
- **Wearable data excluded** (by default): wearable EE error is "substantial, nonrandom, and hard to predict"; weight+intake alone is trusted more (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/26-how-should-i-interpret-changes-to-my-energy-expenditure). 2025's optional "Step-Informed Updates" modifier folds step counts in as *another smoothed input*, never as a daily eat-back adjustment (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/).
- **Metabolic-adaptation bias correction:** tightly controlled studies show weight loss causes ~10–15% TEE reduction beyond mass-based expectation; MacroFactor seeds initial estimates ~10% low when a loss goal is set (BMR equation assumes −5–8% during dieting, plus ~4% per 1% body-weight/week intended loss), and applies a "Predictive Goal Adjustment" nudge when switching goals (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/). This is worth encoding as: on entering a cut phase, expect and display a ~10% downward drift of implied TDEE over the first weeks — it's physiology, not app error.

### 3.5 Carbon Diet Coach — the simpler precedent

Carbon: starting targets via a **modified Müller equation** + body-fat-derived LBM + activity level; **weekly check-in** compares (a) tracked intake vs targets and (b) progress vs target rate of change, then adjusts calories up/down accordingly; if you were non-compliant but still progressing, it may recalibrate to *what you actually ate*; compliance tolerance ±3%/day; weight trend (not single weigh-ins) drives decisions (https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/14005628-how-carbon-sets-and-adjusts-your-macros, https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/6004812-weekly-check-in-in-carbon-how-it-works-and-what-to-expect, https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/5296570-what-is-carbon-and-how-does-the-coaching-system-work). This is a fully **rule-based, offline-recreatable** check-in loop — closer to PersonalOS's weekly check-up than MacroFactor's continuous daily expenditure state. It validates: weekly cadence, target-vs-actual comparison, rate-of-progress adjustments, trend-over-points weighing.

### 3.6 Is PersonalOS's recompute-on-rolling-weight enough? (The verdict)

- **Pure weight-recomputed Mifflin** (recompute equation with new weight) is strictly better than a static number: it tracks weight-driven changes (weight loss → lower TDEE) and removes day-to-day noise via the rolling average. But it **cannot see activity changes or metabolic adaptation** — its PAL multiplier is frozen forever, so a user who starts training harder, or adapts down during a long cut, keeps a stale multiplier; the error stays constant because it's baked into the formula, not into the recompute.
- **An intake-aware model** (implied TDEE = average logged intake − trend-weight-change × 7700/7 adjusted per day) fixes that: it measures the *whole* expenditure (formula errors, PAL error, activity shifts, adaptation) from the user's own data. Given M3 includes food logging and a weekly check-up, the incremental cost is one extra weekly computation.
- **Recommendation:** (1) Ship the locked rolling-weight recompute now (it satisfies B4 and captures most of the value); (2) implement implied-TDEE as an offline, rule-based *insight* at check-up — never auto-applied, surfaced as "your data suggests maintenance ≈ X kcal (from N logged days, trend −0.3 kg/wk)"; the user can adopt it via the manual TDEE override (B4 freeze stays absolute). (3) Encode MacroFactor's guardrails: ≥6/7 logged intake days and ≥3 weigh-ins/wk to compute; pause otherwise; cap suggested change at ±150–250 kcal/wk; two-step commitment (hedge, then confirm); symmetric gain/loss energy content.

## 4. Macro Distribution Science

### 4.1 Protein per phase — validating cut 2.0 / bulk 1.8 / maintain 1.6 (g/kg BW)

**Maintain 1.6:** The best-supported number in the literature. Morton et al. 2018 (BJSM meta, 49 studies, 1,863 participants): RET-induced FFM gains plateau at ~1.62 g/kg/d (95% CI 1.03–2.20); recommendation ~2.2 for those maximizing gains (https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376). ISSN 2017 protein position: 1.4–2.0 g/kg sufficient for most exercising individuals (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8). Tagawa 2020 (105 RCTs, n=5,402): dose-response slopes diminish above ~1.3 g/kg but remain positive up to 3.5 g/kg (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7727026/). → 1.6 sits exactly at the plateau: correct.

**Bulk 1.8:** Conservative but defensible. The evidence window for muscle gain is ~1.6–2.2 g/kg (Morton's upper CI; Schoenfeld & Aragon: 0.4 g/kg × 4 meals = 1.6, up to 0.55 g/kg/meal = 2.2) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/). ISSN 2017: 1.4–2.0 sufficient; intakes >3 g/kg add nothing for most (Antonio's series: 3.3–4.4 g/kg produced no extra gains — no harm, no benefit) (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y). → 1.8 is a reasonable mid-point; 2.0–2.2 is the "maximize" option. Consider making the bulk ceiling 2.0 with an optional 2.2 "hardgainer" setting.

**Cut 2.0:** Well supported, if anything conservative for very lean people. Helms 2014 (systematic review, energy-restricted RT athletes): 2.3–3.1 g/kg **FFM** (≈1.8–2.5 g/kg BW for lean athletes) (https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/24/2/article-p127.xml). 2023 NSCA meta-regression (29 studies): ≥1.9 g/kg BW / 2.5 g/kg FFM associated with less FFM loss; linear dose-response to 3.2 g/kg BW (https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/9900/effect_of_dietary_protein_on_fat_free_mass_in.179.aspx). Aragon 2018: 1.6–2.4 g/kg for elite weight loss, scaled by deficit severity (https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/28/2/article-p170.xml). Mettler 2010: 2.3 vs 1.0 g/kg in a 40% deficit → −0.3 vs −1.6 kg LBM (ibid.). Longland 2016: 2.4 g/kg in a deficit *gained* 1.2 kg LBM while 1.2 g/kg merely maintained (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y). Pasiakos 2013: 1.6 and 2.4 g/kg both preserved FFM vs RDA 0.8 (ibid.). → 2.0 is squarely in the effective range; a note that very lean users (BF <12% M / <20% F) benefit from up to 2.4 g/kg BW, and g/kg **FFM** scaling is the precision upgrade if body fat % is ever captured.

**Nuance to encode:** protein needs scale inversely with body fat % (leaner → higher requirement per kg BW) and with deficit size (deeper deficit → higher end of range) (https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/9900/effect_of_dietary_protein_on_fat_free_mass_in.179.aspx, https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijsnem/28/2/article-p170.xml). Phase values are right; documenting *why* cut > bulk > maintain satisfies the explainable-math rule.

### 4.2 Fat floor 0.6 g/kg — hormonal rationale

- **Low-fat diets reduce testosterone.** Whittaker & Wu meta-analysis (6 studies, 206 men): low-fat (~19%E) vs higher-fat (~39%E) diets reduced total T, free T, DHT, and urinary T, via reduced testicular production; effect ~50–100 ng/dl; largest drops in vegetarian low-fat studies (possible zinc/vitamin-D confounding) (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0960076021000716, https://www.strongerbyscience.com/low-fat-diets-testosterone/).
- **Trexler's evidence-graded floor table** (Stronger by Science, 2023): gallstones 7–12 g/d; essential fatty acids 20–30 g/d; fat-soluble vitamin absorption 20–30 g/d; **sex hormone support 40–60 g/d** — and fat should be set in *absolute grams*, not % of energy (percentage targets break at low calories) (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/dietary-fat/).
- **0.6 g/kg sanity check:** for a 75 kg user that's 45 g/d — inside the 40–60 g sex-hormone band; for a 90 kg user, 54 g. This is a genuine physiological floor, not a round number. Context: physique athletes maintain ~0.6–0.8 g/kg in contest prep (Chappell 2018; Pardue 2017: 0.7 g/kg) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7052702/); Helms 2014 recommends 15–30% of calories for bodybuilders but stresses the deficit/body-fat level, not fat %, drives the hormonal decline (https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20).
- **Caveat to surface in-app:** when calories are very low (deep cut), protein at 2.0 g/kg + fat at 0.6 g/kg may crowd carbs below performance needs; Helms notes higher-carb/low-fat interventions preserved LBM better than low-carb/high-fat during deficits (https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20). The floor is a *floor*: raise it toward 0.8–1.0 g/kg (or 25–30%E) when carbs aren't the constraint (https://denstarfitness.com/how-much-fat-per-day-to-build-muscle/).

### 4.3 Carbs as remainder — logic and evidence

Physiological priority ordering is correct: protein (anabolic + satiety + TEF) and fat (hormonal/EFA floors) are the non-negotiables; carbs take the remainder and are the primary training fuel (glycogen) (https://denstarfitness.com/how-much-fat-per-day-to-build-muscle/, https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20). ISSN 2017: a wide range of fat/carb splits works for body composition; carbs matter most for training performance and LBM retention during deficits (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y). TEF footnote for the explainable-math line: protein ~20–30%, carbs ~5–10%, fat ~0–3% of energy consumed (standard textbook values) — the remainder logic inherently shifts some "waste" energy toward protein.

### 4.4 Per-meal protein distribution (meal pacing)

- **Saturable per-meal dose:** MPS maximized at ~0.24 g/kg (young) to ~0.40 g/kg (older) per meal (Moore et al.; Morton's 0.4 g/kg/meal recommendation = 0.25 + 2 SD) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/, https://examine.com/faq/does-the-distribution-of-protein-intake-throughout-the-day-matter/).
- **Schoenberg/Aragon 2018:** target 0.4 g/kg/meal across ≥4 meals → ≥1.6 g/kg/d; 0.55 g/kg/meal max if chasing 2.2 g/kg/d; larger boluses are partly oxidized but not wasted (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/).
- **Even distribution wins acutely:** Mamerow 2014: 24-h mixed MPS ~25% higher with even 3×~30 g vs skewed (10/16/65 g) (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022316622009087). Yasuda 12-wk RCT: ≥0.24 g/kg at breakfast → significantly more hypertrophy than a protein-skipped breakfast at equal daily totals (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022316622022337). Areta 2014: 20 g every 3 h beat 10 g/1.5 h and 40 g/6 h for whole-body balance (https://eprints.qut.edu.au/64124/1/64124.pdf).
- **But long-term evidence is mixed:** the 2020 MDPI review (Hudson et al.) finds distribution effects limited/inconsistent once total protein is adequate (>1.0 g/kg) — at least one saturating meal/day may suffice (https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/12/5/1441, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7285146/).
- **PersonalOS takeaway:** total daily g/kg is the hard contract; meal pacing (≥0.25–0.4 g/kg × 3–4 meals, breakfast included) is a *soft guidance* tier — a check-up tip, not a target. The macro-gap bar is daily; per-meal pacing can be a per-meal hint in the log (see §7.3).

## 5. Exercise Energy Accuracy

### 5.1 The MET method

The canonical formula `kcal = MET × 3.5 × kg × min / 200` derives from 1 MET = 3.5 mL O₂/kg/min ≈ 1 kcal/kg/h. Three documented problems:

1. **The 1-MET baseline overestimates resting cost by 10–35%** (10–30% per the Copenhagen PAL paper; 35% VO₂ / 20% kcal per the 769-person study; BMI-specific correction factors required) (https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/revisiting-the-calculation-of-pal-from-physical-activity-data/, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4428382/).
2. **The Compendium was built for population classification, not individual prescription** — group-average MET values applied to individuals; in a 769-person heterogeneous sample, resting 1 MET was wrong for essentially everyone (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4428382/).
3. **Wearables that operationalize METs are worse:** Stanford 2017 — seven wrist devices, error 27–93%, best device 27% *over* (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2500062 — companion study; device errors cited at https://nutrola.app/en/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories, https://www.getkalohealth.com/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories); 2016 JAMA IM study — all 12 devices *underestimated* free-living TEE by 69–590 kcal/d (https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2500062); JMIR 2019 (metabolic chamber + DLW) — PAEE errors MAPE 26.5–93.7% (standardized day) and 19.4–100.2% (free-living), most devices significantly off (https://mhealth.jmir.org/2019/8/e13938); O'Driscoll 2018 meta — pooled underestimation, heterogeneity I² >75–90%, no device consistently within a 10% equivalence zone (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327521720_How_well_do_activity_monitors_estimate_energy_expenditure_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_the_validity_of_current_technologies).

**The honest frame for PersonalOS's cardio MET line:** it's a *Compendium-grade estimate*, ±20–50% at the individual level, systematically high for trained/fit people and low-intensity activities (https://nutrola.app/en/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories). Display with error framing, never as an intake credit (see §5.3).

### 5.2 Strength-training estimates (NU9 band)

Evidence for a band, not a point:

- Whole-session values (including rest): ~3–11 kcal/min in men (Mookerjee 2016: single-set ~71 kcal gross vs multi-set ~168 kcal for 5 upper-body exercises; https://www.ovid.com/jnls/nsca-jscr/pdf/10.1519/jsc.0000000000001230~comparison-of-energy-expenditure-during-single-set-vs); ACSM single-set 8-exercise protocol: 135 kcal (men) / 82 kcal (women), ~3.9–4.2 MET (https://doi.org/10.1519/00124278-200305000-00023); heavy-load full-body session ≈289 kcal (4.0 kcal/min) and BodyPump ≈302 kcal (4.7 kcal/min) in overweight women (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2020.00570/full).
- Isolated-exercise range: 3–11.7 kcal/min at low intensity; up to 40 kcal/min for heavy half-squats including anaerobic sources (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3588900/, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0181311).
- **Nuckols' practical heuristic (the band NU9 should mirror):** total energy expenditure during resistance training ≈ **6 kcal/min including rest intervals** (4–8 kcal/min for most people); additive (net) = minutes × (6 − BMR/min); a calculator with low/high bounds is provided (https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-expenditure-resistance/).
- MET-based resistance values (Compendium: 3.5 / 5.0 / 6.0 MET) undercount the glycolytic component; band selection is the hard part; the 2024 scoping review endorses MET as the field-feasible method "with caution" (https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02047-8).

**Recommendation:** implement NU9 as: strength band 3.5–6 MET (Compendium) OR time-based ~4–6 kcal/min net-of-BMR heuristic; display a range with ±25–50% framing; treat as informational only (see §5.3 and §7.5).

### 5.3 The double-count problem — and the macro-gap bar

The canonical failure: TDEE-from-multiplier already embeds assumed exercise; adding exercise kcal on top counts it twice. If the multiplier was set to "moderately active" (1.55), training is already inside the target — eat-back on top erases the deficit (https://www.getkalohealth.com/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories, https://nutrola.app/en/blog/why-eating-back-exercise-calories-is-making-you-gain-weight, https://www.epnutrition.co/blog/eatingbackcaloriesburned, https://mytdeecalculatorpro.com/should-you-eat-back-exercise-calories-tdee/). The arithmetic: watch says 600 kcal, real burn ≈300–450 (27–93% overestimate range); eating 600 back at 4 sessions/wk wipes a 600 kcal weekly deficit (https://fuelnutrition.app/blog/how-to-use-watch-calories-without-eating-back-every-workout). Additionally, exercise can be partially offset by NEAT compensation — "eat-back 100%" compounds that (https://nutrola.app/en/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories). Best-practice consensus for fat loss: eat back 0–50% of device-reported burn, only if the baseline was set "sedentary" (https://www.getkalohealth.com/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories, https://nutrola.app/en/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories). MacroFactor's stance is the cleanest: **no exercise data needed at all** — weight trend + intake already capture the full effect of exercise on expenditure (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/26-how-should-i-interpret-changes-to-my-energy-expenditure).

**Implication for the macro-gap bar:** if the bar is `intake vs calorieTarget` (target embeds PAL), then (a) never auto-expand the target with logged exercise kcal — that is the double-count by construction; (b) show exercise estimates as a *separate informational line* ("this session ≈ 250–350 kcal — ±30% estimate") so the user understands why the bar doesn't move on training days; (c) let the weekly trend be the only thing that moves the target. This keeps the bar honest and the math explainable.

## 6. Weigh-In / TDEE Interaction

### 6.1 How often to weigh

- Daily weighing correlates with better outcomes: n=9,768 smart-scale cohort — only daily weighing associated with negative weight-change-per-day in all BMI groups; breaks ≥30 d → gain (0.58/0.93/1.37 kg by BMI group) (https://www.jmir.org/2021/6/e25529/). WEIGH RCT: daily weighers lost 9.2 kg vs 3.1 kg at 6 months (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4380831/). Breaks >1 week → risk of regain (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0113164).
- Caveat: the RCT evidence (daily vs weekly, controlled) is mixed — Gokee-LaRose found no difference within a strong behavioral program (cited in https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4380831/; Tracking Study protocol: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4314442/). The scale-frequency→outcome link is partly adherence, partly self-monitoring.
- **For PersonalOS's data pipeline, the answer is unambiguous: more weigh-ins = better TDEE estimates.** MacroFactor: daily ideal, ≥3/wk evenly spaced sufficient, weekly works (interpolation + trend); expenditure only needs ~3 weeks of data to refine (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates). PersonalOS should ask for ≥3/wk and recommend daily.

### 6.2 The water-noise problem

Day-to-day variance (sodium, glycogen + bound water ~3 g/g, gut contents, menstrual cycle, stress) makes single weigh-ins near-useless for rate inference: in a 6-week semaglutide trial, individual daily-weight correlations with time averaged only r = −0.64, and regression-on-all-daily-weights halved the error vs 2-point (Day42−Day1) estimates (limits of agreement 1.72–1.26 vs 2.06–9.24 kg/6wk) (https://doi.org/10.2337/db26-2656-p). Free-living weight is genuinely unstable day-to-day while hydration biomarkers stay flat (https://scholarworks.uark.edu/etd/1857). Remedies already in PersonalOS's design: weekly rolling average (equivalent to regression slope over 7 days); MacroFactor's refinement: recency-weighted moving average over a long back-window with linear interpolation of gaps, and a **20-day change rate** for expenditure inference (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/21-weight-trend, https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/19-change-rate). Recommendation: compute the recompute signal from ≥14–20 days of trend, not 7, and require ≥3 weigh-ins/wk.

### 6.3 Signed-rate model vs percentage models

- **Signed additive (PersonalOS):** `deficit/surplus = rate(kg/wk) × 7700 / 7 kcal/d` added to TDEE. Matches the canonical implementation: MacroFactor "converts the desired rate to the size of the calorie surplus/deficit it represents, and adds/subtracts it from expenditure" (https://macrofactor.com/macrofactors-algorithms-and-core-philosophy/); Carbon similarly targets absolute weekly rates (https://help.joincarbon.com/en/articles/6004812-weekly-check-in-in-carbon-how-it-works-and-what-to-expect). It is linear, symmetric (no V2 drift bug, §3.3), and trivially explainable: −0.5 kg/wk → −550 kcal/d.
- **Percentage models (e.g., 1% BW/wk):** scale with body weight — more conservative for light users, more aggressive for heavy ones. The literature's slow-rate guidance is mostly expressed as %/wk (0.5–1%: Helms 2014; slower 0.7% vs 1.4% preserved/gained LBM — Garthe 2011; 1 kg/wk caused testosterone drops in women vs 0.5 kg/wk — Mero 2010) (https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7052702/).
- **Which to ship:** the signed absolute rate is the right core (simple, linear, matches the 7700 estimate framing, canonical form per the contract). Optional guardrail: if a user's rate × 7700/7 exceeds ~30% of TDEE (≈1%+ BW/wk equivalent for most), warn "aggressive — literature associates >1%/wk with greater lean-mass and hormonal cost" and suggest the slower option. This keeps the math signed-additive while importing the %-based safety envelope.

### 6.4 Check-up cadence and the adaptation arc

Weekly check-in is the industry standard (MacroFactor weekly programs; Carbon's weekly check-in is its core loop) and matches a 7-day energy-balance window. Expect an arc: first 1–3 weeks show the "early phase" (glycogen/water) where the 7700 math reads wrong; weeks 3+ converge to the fat-dominated rate; during a long cut, implied TDEE drifts down ~10–15% beyond mass-based expectation (metabolic adaptation; MacroFactor's goal-based seed corrections; https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/, https://www.joincarbon.com/blog/how-to-lose-weight-and-keep-it-off). The check-up copy should teach this arc — it is the #1 cause of users "not trusting the math."

## 7. Steal-Worthy for PersonalOS (recommendations)

1. **Intake-aware "implied TDEE" insight at check-up (future upgrade, offline-recreatable now).** Given M3 food logging: `implied TDEE ≈ avg logged kcal (last 7–14 d) − (trend-weight change × 7700 / days)`. Guardrails copied from MacroFactor: compute only with ≥6/7 logged days and ≥3 weigh-ins/wk, else "insufficient data — holding"; cap suggested delta at ±150–250 kcal/wk; hedge week 1, commit week 2; symmetric gain/loss energy content; surface as insight, apply only via manual TDEE (B4 freeze intact) (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/dashboard/expenditure, https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-v3/). Why: rolling-weight Mifflin recompute can't see PAL error, activity shifts, or metabolic adaptation; intake-aware fixes all three with one weekly formula — the biggest accuracy lever after the equation itself.

2. **Error framing on every derived number (explainable math, made honest).** Label targets with their confidence: "TDEE from Mifflin-St Jeor: ±10–15% typical error (±200–350 kcal for you) — refines as your weight data accumulates" (https://www.clinicalnutritionjournal.com/article/S0261-5614(13)00100-3/abstract, https://tdeecalculator.net/faq.php); "7,700 kcal/kg = fat-tissue energy density — expect ±30–40% divergence in early weeks/water swings" (§2); "exercise kcal ±25–50% estimate" (§5). This converts the app's biggest weakness (formula error) into a trust-building feature.

3. **Protein meal-pacing as soft guidance.** Keep g/kg/day as the contract; add a check-up tip when daily protein is hit but skewed: "aim for ~0.25–0.4 g/kg per meal across 3–4 meals (≈20–40 g); even distribution supports ~25% higher 24-h muscle synthesis" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5828430/, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022316622009087). Never a hard target — long-term outcome evidence is mixed (https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/12/5/1441).

4. **Fat floor in absolute grams with rationale, plus a carb-crowding warning.** Keep 0.6 g/kg floor (45 g @ 75 kg — inside the 40–60 g/d sex-hormone band; Trexler's floor table: https://www.strongerbyscience.com/dietary-fat/). When a deep cut would leave carbs very low, surface: "consider raising fat toward 0.8–1.0 g/kg; very-low-carb deficits are associated with worse training performance and LBM retention" (https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20). Absolute grams, never % — percentage floors break at low calories.

5. **Exercise kcal: display-only, never bar-expanding (double-count proof by construction).** The macro-gap bar compares intake vs calorieTarget; calorieTarget already embeds the PAL multiplier, so adding exercise to the bar double-counts (https://www.getkalohealth.com/blog/should-i-eat-back-exercise-calories). Ship: cardio MET line (`MET × 3.5 × kg × min / 200`) and NU9 strength band (3.5–6 MET or ~6 kcal/min net heuristic: https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-expenditure-resistance/) as *informational lines with error framing*; a one-line explainer: "your target already assumes this training; the weekly trend is the only adjustment authority." This is exactly MacroFactor's no-exercise-data philosophy, minus the complexity (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/26-how-should-i-interpret-changes-to-my-energy-expenditure).

6. **Weigh-in policy and trend signal.** Nudge ≥3–5 weigh-ins/wk (daily ideal; data richness directly improves the recompute; https://www.jmir.org/2021/6/e25529/, https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/109-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-weight-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates). Recompute TDEE from a **20-day trend**, not 7 (MacroFactor's change-rate window; https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/19-change-rate); interpolate gaps linearly; hold recompute when <3 weigh-ins/wk (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/21-weight-trend). The weekly check-up should teach the adaptation arc (early water phase → fat-dominated phase → ~10–15% downward drift on long cuts; https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/) so users don't distrust the math exactly when it's right.

7. **Phase-entry expectation-setting (bonus).** On entering a cut: pre-announce "your body will fight the deficit — expect implied TDEE to drift ~10% lower over the first weeks; that's physiology, not a bug" (https://macrofactor.com/expenditure-modifiers/, https://www.joincarbon.com/blog/how-to-lose-weight-and-keep-it-off). On bulk entry, expect the mirror image (a transient upward read as glycogen loads). One line each, at the phase screen — cheap, and prevents the most common churn point in adaptive systems.
