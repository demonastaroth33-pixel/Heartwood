# A. Seed: Dicot Seed Biology, Germination & Seedling Establishment

*Research brief for the Life Tree growth engine. Scope: dicots (flowering plants with two seed leaves) — trees (oak, maple) plus garden/agricultural examples (bean, pea, tomato, sunflower). Monocots and gymnosperms appear only as contrast; fruit types colloquially called "seeds" are flagged where relevant.*

A **seed** — the mature, fertilized ovule: a dormant miniature plant (the embryo) wrapped in stored food and a protective coat, able to survive until conditions favor growth.

## 1. Seed structure

### 1.1 The seed coat (testa) and surface features
- **Testa** — the seed coat, the outer protective shell of the seed, formed from the ovule's integuments (§2.1); usually hard, often waterproof and pigmented.
- **Tegmen** — the inner layer of the seed coat, formed from the inner integument; the testa is the outer layer. In many seeds the two fuse into one shell.
- **Hilum** — the scar left on the seed coat where the seed was attached to its stalk, the **funiculus** (the stalk connecting the ovule to the ovary wall); the oval mark on a bean or pea seed.
- **Micropyle** — the tiny pore at one end of the seed, the original entry point of the pollen tube during fertilization; still visible beside the hilum on bean seeds; the main water-entry point during imbibition (§4.1) and the usual exit point of the radicle (§5).
- **Raphe** — a ridge running along the seed surface where the funiculus fused to the ovule wall (in inverted "anatropous" ovules, §2.2); visible on bean seeds.
- **Chalaza** — the point at the far end of the ovule where the integuments meet the nucellus, opposite the micropyle.
- **Lens / strophiole** — a small raised valve-like area near the hilum in many hard-coated seeds; it pops open when the coat is scarified (§3.6), letting water in.

### 1.2 The embryo
- **Embryo** — the miniature plant inside the seed (the new sporophyte generation — the familiar 2n plant body). It consists of:
  - **Radicle** — the embryonic root, the first organ to emerge at germination (§5); becomes the primary root / taproot.
  - **Plumule** — the embryonic shoot, the first bud; carries the first true leaves and becomes the shoot's growing tip.
  - **Hypocotyl** — the embryonic stem region below the cotyledons (between radicle and cotyledons).
  - **Epicotyl** — the embryonic stem region above the cotyledons (between cotyledons and plumule).
  - **Cotyledons** — the seed leaves; the first leaves of the embryo. Dicots have exactly two ("dicot" = two cotyledons). They store food (§1.3), may emerge above ground and photosynthesize (epigeal germination, §5), or stay underground as a nutrient depot (hypogeal, §5).

### 1.3 Food storage: endosperm vs cotyledons
- **Endosperm** — the nutritive tissue formed by double fertilization (§2.2); triploid (3n). In some seeds it is consumed by the growing embryo before maturity; in others it persists as the main storage tissue.
- **Cotyledonary storage** — reserves (starch, oil, protein) packed directly into the cotyledons instead of an endosperm.
- **Albuminous seeds** — retain endosperm at maturity: tomato, pepper, castor bean (Ricinus — a huge oily endosperm wrapped around thin papery cotyledons), coffee, cotton, papaya.
- **Exalbuminous seeds** — endosperm fully absorbed during development; the cotyledons are the storage organs: bean, pea, sunflower, oak, walnut.
- **Perisperm** — food stored in persisting nucellus tissue (§2.1) rather than endosperm or cotyledons (beet, black pepper).

### 1.4 Worked anatomy examples
- **Bean (Phaseolus vulgaris)** — exalbuminous: brown testa with a clear hilum and micropyle at one end; two massive fleshy cotyledons (starch); between them the embryo axis — the pointed radicle at the micropyle end and the plumule tucked inside with two small folded true leaves. Epigeal germination (§5).
- **Pea (Pisum sativum)** — same plan (hilum, micropyle, two big cotyledons) but hypogeal germination (§5): the cotyledons never see daylight.
- **Sunflower** — the "seed" you plant is an achene (§2.3), a one-seeded dry fruit; the true seed inside is exalbuminous with oily cotyledons.

## 2. Seed types and origins

### 2.1 How a seed forms (fertilization recap)
Inside the flower's **ovary** (the hollow female reproductive organ that becomes the fruit) sit one or more **ovules** (the structures that become seeds). Each ovule has: a **funiculus** (stalk), two **integuments** (protective coats that become the testa), the **nucellus** (the tissue mass inside the coats), and inside it the **embryo sac** — the female gametophyte, the egg-bearing structure (typically 7 cells: egg cell, two synergids, three antipodals, and a central cell with two polar nuclei — the accessory cells support the egg; the polar nuclei will form endosperm). A pollen grain lands on the **stigma** (the pollen-catching tip of the female organ), grows a **pollen tube** (a cellular tube delivering sperm) down the **style** (the neck of the female organ) into the ovary, and enters the ovule through the micropyle, releasing two sperm cells.

### 2.2 Double fertilization
- **Double fertilization** — the defining event of flowering plants: one sperm fuses with the **egg cell** (the female gamete) → **zygote** (fertilized egg, 2n) → embryo; the other sperm fuses with the central cell's two **polar nuclei** → **endosperm** (3n).
- Afterward the ovule becomes the seed: integuments → testa; nucellus usually absorbed (or persisting as perisperm); the zygote divides through **proembryo** (early cell mass) → globular → heart → torpedo stages to form the mature two-cotyledon embryo; the ovary wall becomes the **pericarp** (fruit wall).
- **Ovule orientation** explains surface features: **anatropous** (inverted, commonest — bean; produces the raphe), **orthotropous** (straight), **campylotropous** (curved — mustards), **amphitropous** (half-inverted).
- Origin variants: **apomixis** — seeds without fertilization (dandelion); **polyembryony** — one seed, several embryos (citrus).

### 2.3 Seed vs fruit — the terminology trap
Many "seeds" are actually fruits: **achene** (one-seeded dry fruit whose wall is NOT fused to the seed coat — sunflower), **samara** (winged achene — maple, ash, elm, §8.1), **drupe** (fleshy fruit with a stony inner wall — peach, cherry, olive; the **endocarp** is the stone, the "pit"), **berry** (fleshy fruit with seeds in pulp — tomato, grape), **nut** (hard-shelled one-seeded fruit — oak acorn, hazel).

## 3. Dormancy

**Dormancy** — a *viable* (alive, capable of germinating) seed refusing to germinate even when water, temperature, and oxygen are all favorable. An evolved brake, not a defect. Contrast **quiescence** — merely waiting for water (normal for dry-stored seeds); quiescence ends the moment water arrives, dormancy does not.

**Why dormancy evolved:** bet-hedging (spreading the risk of one bad year across years), seasonal timing (never germinate just before winter or the dry season), gap detection (germinate only where light and space exist), predator escape (staggered germination), and within-season spread (not all seeds sprout at once even in a good year).

The standard classification (Baskin & Baskin) recognizes five classes:

### 3.1 Physical dormancy (PY)
- The seed coat is impermeable to water: a **palisade layer** (a dense sheet of elongated, dead, lignified cells called **macrosclereids**) blocks entry. The embryo is fully developed and non-dormant — it just can't get wet.
- Broken by **scarification** (§3.6); the water gap (lens/strophiole, §1.1) pops open.
- Examples: morning glory, wisteria, black locust, honey locust, lupine, clovers, lotus (Nelumbo), the mallow family.

### 3.2 Physiological dormancy (PD)
- The most common class. Embryo fully developed, coat water-permeable, but growth is blocked by hormone balance: high **ABA (abscisic acid)** — the hormone that maintains dormancy and blocks germination — versus **gibberellins (GAs)** — growth-promoting hormones that overcome ABA and activate the enzymes that mobilize stored food. Dormant = high ABA/low GA; released = low ABA/high GA.
- **After-ripening** — dormancy declines during dry storage at room temperature (weeks–months): sunflower achenes, many mustards.
- **Cold stratification** (§3.6) mimics winter: apple, maple, ash, beech, most temperate trees. Oak splits the class: **white oak** acorns have *no* dormancy (germinate in autumn at shed); **red oak** acorns have deep PD needing ~3 months of cold.
- Light-requiring dormancy is a PD variant (§4.3).

### 3.3 Morphological dormancy (MD)
- The embryo is *underdeveloped* at seed shed (often less than a quarter of the seed's length — tiny, sometimes barely differentiated) and must **grow to full size inside the seed** before the radicle can emerge. No hormone block — just incomplete development, finished by warm moist conditions or time.
- Examples: pawpaw (Asimina), Campanula americana, many palms, many parsley/buttercup-family herbs.

### 3.4 Morphophysiological dormancy (MPD)
- MD + PD combined: an underdeveloped embryo that is ALSO physiologically dormant. Usually a two-step treatment — **warm stratification** (moist warmth lets the embryo grow) then **cold stratification** (breaks the hormone block) — or the reverse order in some species.
- Examples: ginseng (Panax), pawpaw, holly (Ilex), ash (Fraxinus), celery, Trillium, tree peony, many palms.

### 3.5 Combinational dormancy (PY + PD)
- A waterproof coat AND an embryo with physiological dormancy; needs scarification AND stratification.
- Examples: redbud (Cercis canadensis), Ceanothus, several Geranium species, Rosa rugosa.

### 3.6 Breaking dormancy: stratification and scarification
- **Scarification** — any treatment that weakens or opens the seed coat so water can enter: mechanical (nicking, filing, sandpaper), hot water, concentrated acid (sulfuric), fire/heat. Nature's equivalents: soil abrasion, animal guts, fire, freeze-thaw cracking.
- **Stratification** — moist storage at a set temperature that mimics a season: **cold stratification** (1–5 °C, 1–4 months — mimics winter; apple, maple, red oak, peach pits), **warm stratification** (20–30 °C moist — lets underdeveloped embryos mature; the first step for MPD species). Some MPD seeds need warm→cold or cold→warm sequences.

### 3.7 Seed banks
- **Seed bank** — the population of viable, ungerminated seeds lying in the soil (or on it). **Transient** banks germinate within a year; **persistent** banks last years to decades. Dormancy type determines bank behavior; burial often adds a light requirement (§4.3), so disturbance (plowing, fire, landslides) triggers mass germination.
- **Serotiny** — canopy seed banking: fire-adapted plants (some pines, Banksia) hold seeds on the plant for years until fire opens the cones/fruits — a fire-triggered "bank" above ground.

## 4. Germination triggers

**Germination** — the resumption of embryo growth ending in **radicle emergence** (the root tip poking through the coat); everything after that is seedling growth (§5–6). A seed germinates only when ALL requirements are met: water, temperature, oxygen, and (for some species) light, fire, or specific chemistry.

### 4.1 Water: imbibition
- **Imbibition** — the physical uptake of water by dry seed tissues (starches, proteins, and colloids swell). Three phases:
  - **Phase I** (minutes–hours): rapid physical uptake; the seed swells, the testa may split, respiration reactivates.
  - **Phase II** (plateau): slow water gain while metabolism revs up — DNA/RNA repair, protein synthesis, enzyme activation, and the hormone flip (ABA falls, GA rises).
  - **Phase III**: water uptake resumes as the radicle elongates and emerges — germination completes.
- Too much water = oxygen starvation (§4.4); too little = the seed stalls in phase II.

### 4.2 Temperature
- **Cardinal temperatures** — the three benchmark temperatures per species: **base/minimum** (below it, no germination — bean ~10 °C), **optimum** (fastest germination — bean 25–30 °C), **maximum** (above it, damage or inhibition — bean ~35–40 °C). Most crop seeds sit in the 20–35 °C optimum band.
- **Thermoinhibition** — high temperature blocks germination even with water present (lettuce above ~25 °C).
- **Alternating temperatures** (day/night fluctuation) promote germination in many species (weeds, some trees) — a cue of bare, open soil.
- Chilling-then-warming sequencing (stratification §3.6) is how temperate seeds wait for spring.

### 4.3 Light: photoblasty
- **Photoblastic** — germination controlled by light. **Positive photoblastic**: light required or strongly promoting (lettuce, tobacco, birch, most small-seeded pioneers); **negative photoblastic**: darkness preferred, light inhibits (onion, Nigella, lily, Phacelia tanacetifolia); **non-photoblastic / aphotoblastic**: indifferent (bean, pea, most large seeds).
- **Phytochrome** — the light-sensing pigment, in two interconvertible forms: **Pr** (red-absorbing, inactive) and **Pfr** (far-red-absorbing, active). Red light converts Pr→Pfr (promotes germination); far-red converts Pfr→Pr (inhibits); the last light exposure wins.
- Ecological meaning: leafy canopies filter out red light, so shade is far-red-rich → Pfr falls → seeds stay dormant; a light gap, bare soil, or disturbance lets red through → germination. This is "gap detection" and why small pioneer seeds must stay near the surface.
- Positive photoblastic seeds often need only a brief light pulse after imbibition.

### 4.4 Oxygen
- Germination runs on aerobic respiration; waterlogged or compacted soil starves seeds, and deep sowing kills light-requiring seeds both by darkness and low oxygen.

### 4.5 Fire and smoke: karrikins
- **Karrikins** — butenolide compounds in wood smoke (e.g., **karrikinolide KAR1**) that trigger germination in fire-following species of Australia, South Africa, and California (many daisy-family species; smoke water is sold commercially for horticulture).
- Fire also breaks physical dormancy by cracking hard coats with heat — two independent fire cues working together in fire-adapted floras.

### 4.6 Other promoters and inhibitors
- **Nitrate** (from decaying organic matter), **ethylene** (a gas from ripening fruit and soil microbes), and CO₂ promote germination in some species.
- Germination inhibitors in fruit pulp: tomato juice suppresses its own seeds until the fruit ferments or rots (or passes through an animal gut) — a timing device.

### 4.7 Seed longevity and viability
- **Viability** — a seed is alive and capable of germinating; **longevity** — how long viability lasts under given storage.
- **Orthodox seeds** — tolerate drying and cold storage; most crop and temperate tree seeds (bean, tomato, maple); these are what seed banks store.
- **Recalcitrant seeds** — die if dried; they stay moist and short-lived: oak acorns, horse chestnut, avocado, mango, cacao, rubber tree. This is why oaks must germinate fast — they cannot be seed-banked.
- **Intermediate** seeds sit between (some citrus).
- Longevity records: ~1,300-year-old lotus (Nelumbo) seeds germinated; ~2,000-year-old date-palm seeds from Masada (Israel) germinated.

### 4.8 Seasonal timing
- **Spring germinators** — need a winter of cold first (red oak, maple, apple).
- **Autumn germinators** — no dormancy or short PD: white oak germinates at shed in autumn; winter annuals (some mustards, speedwells) germinate in autumn, overwinter as **rosettes** (flat circles of leaves hugging the ground), and flower in spring.
- Arid-land seeds germinate only after a sufficiently large rain event — a rain-threshold trigger.

## 5. The germination process, step by step

1. **Imbibition** (§4.1) — water in; the seed swells; the testa splits.
2. **Metabolic awakening** — respiration spikes; enzymes mobilize stored food; ABA falls, GA rises; the radicle's cells begin elongating.
3. **Radicle emergence** — the radicle pushes out, usually through the micropyle (bean) or a crack in the testa. It grows downward by **positive geotropism** (also called gravitropism — growth toward gravity, sensed by **statoliths**, starch granules that settle in the root-cap cells). **Root hairs** (tiny epidermal outgrowths that massively expand the root's surface for water and mineral uptake) form behind the tip.
4. **Hypocotyl vs epicotyl extension — the fork in the road:**
   - **Epigeal germination** — the hypocotyl elongates and drags the cotyledons above ground: bean, sunflower, tomato, maple, most dicots. The hypocotyl first forms a **hook** (the crook/arch) by differential growth; the hook drags the cotyledons and plumule through the soil, protecting the delicate plumule tip from abrasion. Light then breaks the hook (part of **photomorphogenesis** — the light-triggered switch from pale, elongated dark-growth to green, expanded light-growth).
   - **Hypogeal germination** — the epicotyl elongates instead; the cotyledons stay underground as storage organs: pea, oak, walnut, horse chestnut, hazel. A **plumular hook** (an arch in the epicotyl) protects the plumule; the buried cotyledons keep feeding the seedling for weeks to months.
5. **Cotyledon emergence** — epigeal: the cotyledons unfold above ground, green up, and act as the first photosynthetic organs ("seed leaves"); hypogeal: nothing emerges but the shoot tip — the first above-ground organs are true leaves.
6. **First true leaves** — expanded from the plumule; they differ from cotyledons (bean: cotyledons simple and fleshy, first true leaf compound-trifoliate with three leaflets). Photosynthesis now covers running costs; the seedling is self-sufficient.
- **Etiolation** — growth in darkness: the seedling stretches pale, weak, and elongated (**skotomorphogenesis**, the dark-growth morphology) and burns reserves; light reverses it.
- Note the boundary: germination is formally complete at radicle emergence; everything after is **seedling establishment** (§6).

## 6. Seedling establishment

### 6.1 Seedling zones (bottom to top)
- **Primary root** — the original root grown from the radicle; in most dicots it becomes a **taproot** (a single dominant, deep main root — bean, oak).
- **Lateral roots** — branch roots arising from the **pericycle** (an inner tissue layer of the root), formed in sequence toward the root tip; together with root hairs they build the foraging system.
- **Hypocotyl** — the stem stretch below the cotyledons (long in epigeal seedlings).
- **Cotyledons** (epigeal only, above ground) — seed leaves, often the first photosynthetic organs.
- **Epicotyl** — stem above the cotyledons.
- **Plumule** — now the shoot apical meristem (the growing tip) producing the first true leaves.
- **Node** — a leaf-attachment point; **internode** — the stem between two nodes.
- **Adventitious roots** — roots arising from stems or leaves instead of the radicle (willow cuttings, ivy) — not the normal seedling route.

### 6.2 Juvenile vs adult leaves: heteroblasty
- **Heteroblasty** — leaf shape and size change as a plant ages (juvenile phase → adult phase), a phenomenon called **phase change**. Examples: English ivy (juvenile lobed, climbing leaves vs adult unlobed leaves on the flowering shrub), eucalyptus (juvenile round, blue-grey leaves vs adult lanceolate — lance-shaped — leaves), acacia (seedling true leaves replaced by **phyllodes** — flattened, leaf-like petioles), many oaks (juvenile leaves less lobed), citrus (juvenile shoots thorny). A tree seedling's leaves are often NOT the adult's leaves.

### 6.3 Root system development
- Primary root → taproot; lateral roots ramify outward and downward. **Mycorrhizae** — beneficial fungi that partner with roots (the fungus trades water and mineral nutrients, especially phosphorus, for sugars); most trees are obligate partners (oak seedlings without their fungal partner grow poorly).

### 6.4 Seedling vigor
- **Vigor** — a seedling's capacity for fast, uniform, robust growth; driven by seed size and reserves (bigger acorns → bigger seedlings), maternal environment, seed age/storage history, and genetics. The cotyledon (or endosperm) reserve fuels early growth; the "self-sufficiency point" arrives when photosynthesis covers all costs.

### 6.5 Seedling mortality — the most dangerous life stage
- **Drought** (shallow roots), **shade** (light starvation — many trees need a canopy gap), **herbivory** (slugs, snails, insects, rodents, deer), **pathogens** — especially **damping-off**, fungi (Pythium, Rhizoctonia) that rot the stem at soil level so seedlings topple, **frost heaving** (ice crystals lift seedlings out of the soil), **litter burial**, **flooding**, **fire**, and **competition** (grass and litter).
- **Janzen-Connell effect** — seed/seedling specialists kill offspring disproportionately near the parent tree, keeping forests diverse (distance- and density-dependent mortality).
- **Shade tolerance** — many trees (beech, sugar maple) can persist as suppressed seedlings for years in the understory, forming a **seedling bank** (a pool of suppressed seedlings waiting for a canopy gap).

### 6.6 Seedling vs mature morphology
- Leaf shape/arrangement (heteroblasty, §6.2), no bark yet (the **periderm** — protective cork layer — develops later), thin flexible stems, no flowers or reproductive structures, often different hairiness (**pubescence**) and color (many seedlings are reddish — anthocyanin pigments — before greening), and different growth habit (juvenile ivy climbs; adult is a shrub).

## 7. Seed-to-seedling chronological timeline

### 7.1 Warm-season annual — bean, day by day (soil ~25 °C)
- **Day 0** — sown; imbibition begins; the seed swells within hours.
- **Day 1–2** — testa splits; metabolism revs up (phase II).
- **Day 2–4** — radicle emerges through the micropyle; root hairs appear (phase III).
- **Day 4–6** — hypocotyl hook emerges from the soil; lateral roots begin.
- **Day 5–8** — cotyledons above ground; the hook straightens in light; cotyledons green up; plumule visible between them.
- **Day 7–10** — first true (trifoliate) leaves expand; epicotyl elongates.
- **Day 10–14** — second and third trifoliates; root system ramifies.
- **Week 3–4** — cotyledons shrivel and drop (reserves spent); plant fully autotrophic (self-feeding by photosynthesis); ~30 cm tall.

### 7.2 Small-seeded annual — tomato
- Slower start (tiny reserves): radicle day 3–5, hook day 6–10, cotyledons day 8–14, true leaves day 14–21; needs warmth (20–25 °C). Same epigeal sequence, stretched over a longer calendar because the seed is small.

### 7.3 Tree, big seed, hypogeal — oak
- **Autumn shed**: white-oak acorns germinate immediately — the radicle pushes out within days–weeks and grows a deep taproot; the epicotyl stays dormant through winter. Red-oak acorns need winter cold first; the radicle emerges in spring.
- **Spring**: the epicotyl elongates and the first true leaves expand; the cotyledons stay underground, feeding the seedling for the entire first season (reserves can last 1–2+ years).
- **Year 1**: single unbranched stem, 10–30 cm; the root system outweighs the shoot; leaves fall in autumn (deciduous — sheds leaves each autumn).
- **Year 2–5**: branching begins; the seedling must either find a light gap or persist suppressed (seedling bank, §6.5).

### 7.4 Tree, small seed, epigeal, needs cold — maple
- **Winter**: samaras lie in the soil, cold-stratifying.
- **Spring** (soil above ~5–10 °C): imbibition → germination over 1–3 weeks; cotyledons emerge, then true leaves by late spring.
- **Year 1**: seedling 5–20 cm, shade-tolerant; can persist in the understory for years.

## 8. Special seeds

### 8.1 Winged seeds and samaras
- **Samara** — a winged one-seeded dry fruit (the wing is fruit wall, not seed coat): maple's double samara (two winged halves that spin — **autorotation** — like a helicopter, slowing the fall), ash's single samara, elm's samara.
- **True winged seeds** — the seed coat itself bears wings: catalpa (seeds with papery fringes inside bean-like pods), trumpet creeper. **Poplar/cottonwood** seeds are tiny seeds with cottony hairs (also wind-dispersed).
- Wing function: slow the fall so wind carries the seed farther; the trade-off is small seed size (fewer reserves).

### 8.2 Fleshy-fruit seeds
- **Tomato** — seeds in gelatinous coats with germination inhibitors in the pulp; they germinate only after the fruit rots/ferments or passes through an animal gut.
- **Grape** — tough seed coat; often needs cold; bird gut passage aids germination.
- **Peach / cherry / olive (drupes)** — the stony endocarp ("pit") protects the seed; pits need cold stratification, sometimes over two winters; bird passage can help (cherry).
- **Mistletoe** — seeds coated in **viscin** (a sticky glue) that must land on a host branch; bird-dispersed.
- **Strawberry** — the "seeds" on the surface are hundreds of achenes (true fruits) on a swollen receptacle (the red fleshy part); each achene holds one tiny seed.
- Common fleshy-fruit seed traits: tough coats (digestion resistance), pulp inhibitors (timing), and many small seeds per fruit (risk spread).

### 8.3 Dispersal-trait seeds (traits only)
- **Wind (anemochory)** — light weight, wings, hairs, or dust-like tiny seeds (heather — germinates on disturbed peat).
- **Water (hydrochory)** — buoyancy (air spaces, fibrous coats) and waterproof coats in riparian/coastal plants; extreme case: **vivipary** in mangroves (Rhizophora, a dicot) — the "seed" (a **propagule** — a seedling that germinates while still attached to the parent) drops like a dart and roots in mud.
- **Animal — endozoochory** (eaten, defecated): fleshy fruits; seed traits = resistant coats, germination often enhanced by gut passage (acids and abrasion lightly scarify): holly, hawthorn, bird cherry, blackberry (an aggregate fruit of tiny drupelets).
- **Animal — epizoochory** (carried on fur): hooks and spines — burdock burs (hook-tipped), beggar's-lice, stickseed.
- **Animal — myrmecochory** (ants): the seed carries an **elaiosome** — a lipid-rich food body; ants haul the seed, eat the elaiosome, and discard the seed in or near the nest (nutrient-rich refuse, safe from fire and predators): trillium, bloodroot (Sanguinaria), violets, wild ginger.

### 8.4 Seed predation
- **Pre-dispersal predation** — insects eat developing seeds inside fruits: acorn weevils (larvae inside acorns), bruchid beetles in legume seeds.
- **Post-dispersal predation** — **granivores** (seed-eating animals: mice, squirrels, birds, ants) take most seeds; predation is the biggest single cause of seed death.
- **Masting (mast seeding)** — synchronized bumper seed years every few years (oaks, beech, hickory): **predator satiation** — predators gorge in mast years and starve in between, so some seeds always escape.
- **Caching (scatter-hoarding)** — squirrels and jays bury seeds one by one; forgotten caches germinate — effective "planting" that can move acorns kilometers. Some "predators" are thus also dispersers.

*End of brief. Cross-cutting note for the growth engine: seed fate = viability (was it alive?) → dormancy (is it allowed to germinate?) → trigger (did the right signals arrive?) → germination mode (epigeal vs hypogeal) → establishment (does it survive the seedling bottleneck). Each step has its own clock and failure modes.*
