# DICOT STEM BIOLOGY — Research Brief (B-stems)

Master reference for the "Life Tree" growth engine. Covers the shoot system, primary structure, meristems, secondary growth, bark, reaction wood, wood properties, stem modifications, branching, the trunk layer stack, and growth patterns. Every technical term is defined inline in plain language.

---

## 1. The Shoot System — What a Stem Is and Does

A **stem** is the usually above-ground organ of a vascular plant that bears leaves, buds, flowers, and fruits. It is divided into **nodes** (the points where leaves attach) and **internodes** (the stretches between nodes). The stem, its leaves, and its buds together form the **shoot system**, in contrast to the **root system** below ground.

### Stem functions
- **Support** — stems hold leaves up to the light, flowers up to pollinators, and fruits up to dispersers. The trunk of an oak, the cane of a rose, and the twining vine of a grape are all stems doing this job.
- **Transport** — the stem is the highway between roots and leaves: **xylem** (water-conducting tissue) carries water and dissolved minerals upward; **phloem** (sugar-conducting tissue) carries the products of photosynthesis downward and throughout the plant.
- **Storage** — stems store starch, sugars, and water. Sugar maple stores starch in trunk parenchyma; the saguaro cactus stores tons of water in its stem; the potato is a storage stem (see §8).
- **Photosynthesis** — young green stems photosynthesize (chlorophyll lives in the **chlorenchyma** — photosynthetic parenchyma — of the cortex, §2). In cacti and many succulents, the stem is the *only* photosynthetic organ, because the leaves have been reduced to spines.
- **Defense, propagation, perennation** — thorns deter herbivores; runners and rhizomes spread the plant vegetatively; underground stems (bulbs, corms, rhizomes) carry the plant through winter or drought (survival = **perennation**).

### The shoot apical meristem (SAM)
The **shoot apical meristem (SAM)** is a small dome of undifferentiated (unspecialized) cells at the very tip of every shoot. It is the engine of **primary growth** — the lengthening of the stem. It continuously produces new cells: some stay in the meristem, others become **leaf primordia** (the earliest beginnings of leaves, visible as tiny bumps on the dome's flank), **bud primordia** (beginnings of axillary buds), and stem tissue. The SAM in most dicots shows a **tunica-corpus** organization: an outer **tunica** (one or more surface layers that divide in a way that keeps the surface intact) wrapping an inner **corpus** (a mass of cells dividing in all directions).

### Node vs internode
- **Node** — the point on the stem where one or more leaves (and their axillary buds) attach; leaves, tendrils, and flowers always arise at nodes.
- **Internode** — the stem region between two nodes. Internode length determines whether a stem looks "leggy" (long internodes) or "rosetted/compressed" (very short internodes, as in a cabbage or lettuce).

### Buds
A **bud** is a compact, undeveloped shoot — a tiny SAM plus embryonic leaves — wrapped in protective coverings. Types:
- **Axillary (lateral) bud** — a bud in the **leaf axil** (the angle between leaf and stem). It can remain dormant for years, grow into a branch, or become a flower.
- **Terminal (apical) bud** — the bud at the shoot tip; it extends the main stem.
- **Adventitious bud** — a bud arising anywhere other than the shoot tip or leaf axil (e.g., on roots, on internodes, in old bark). Adventitious buds are why a cut willow stump resprouts and why **epicormic shoots** (see §9) burst from trunks.

### Leaf scars, bundle scars, bud scale scars
- **Leaf scar** — the flat mark left on a twig where a leaf fell off. Inside it you can see **bundle scars**: small dots that are the cut ends of the vascular bundles that fed the leaf.
- **Bud scale scars** — rings of tiny scars left when the scale leaves of a winter terminal bud fall off each spring. Counting them from the tip backward ages a twig in years — one ring per year of growth.
- **Lenticel** — a small, corky, pore-like spot on stems (and some roots) made of loosely packed cells with big air spaces; it allows gas exchange (oxygen in, carbon dioxide out) through an otherwise impermeable surface. Birch twigs and cherry bark show them clearly.

### Bud scales
**Bud scales** are small, tough, often resinous modified leaves that protect the dormant SAM inside a winter bud from cold and drying. When growth resumes in spring, the scales drop off, leaving the bud-scale scars described above.

## 2. Primary Structure of a DICOT Stem

"**Primary**" refers to tissue produced by the apical meristems (lengthening). A cross-section of a young dicot stem, from outside to inside, shows: **epidermis → cortex → vascular bundles in a ring → pith**, with **medullary rays** running between the bundles.

### Epidermis
The **epidermis** is the outermost single layer of protective cells. It is covered by the **cuticle** — a waxy, waterproof film (made of **cutin**) that limits water loss. Young stems bear **stomata** (microscopic pores, each flanked by two guard cells, through which gas exchange and water loss happen — the same structures as on leaves) and often **trichomes** (epidermal hairs, which can reflect light, deter insects, or trap moisture).

### Cortex
The **cortex** is the ground tissue between the epidermis and the vascular ring, made of several cell types:
- **Parenchyma** — thin-walled, living, general-purpose cells; store starch and water, and can become meristematic again if needed.
- **Collenchyma** — cells with extra-thick cellulose at the *corners* only; they stay alive and flexible, providing stretchy support to young stems. The "strings" of celery are collenchyma.
- **Chlorenchyma** — chlorophyll-containing parenchyma; this is what makes young stems green and photosynthetic.
- **Sclerenchyma** (sometimes) — dead, very thick-walled cells (**fibers** — long and slender; **sclereids** — short and varied in shape). They give toughness; hemp and flax fibers come from stem sclerenchyma.

### Vascular bundles in a ring (the dicot hallmark)
The **vascular bundles** are the discrete strands of xylem + phloem that run lengthwise through the stem. In dicots they are arranged in a **ring** (visible in cross-section), leaving cortex outside and pith inside. Each bundle is:
- **Collateral** — xylem on the *inside* (toward the pith), phloem on the *outside* (toward the cortex), in the same radius.
- **Open** — a strip of **vascular cambium** (see §3–4) lies between the xylem and phloem, so the bundle can grow outward in girth. This openness is exactly what permits **secondary growth** (thickening) — it is the single most important structural difference between dicot and monocot stems.

### Pith
The **pith** is the central cylinder of ground tissue, mostly parenchyma, used for storage. In some dicots the pith dies and dries out, leaving a hollow stem (elder *Sambucus*, hydrangea, many herbs). In others it persists and becomes part of the heartwood's core (see §10).

### Medullary rays
**Medullary rays** (or pith rays) are sheets of parenchyma that run radially — like the spokes of a wheel — between adjacent vascular bundles, connecting pith to cortex. They store food and, crucially, later supply the cells that form part of the vascular cambium and the **rays in wood** (see §4).

### Endodermis-like starch sheath
The innermost layer of the cortex, just outside the vascular ring, is often a **starch sheath** — a single layer of cells packed with starch granules. It resembles the **endodermis** (the waterproof boundary layer of roots) and is sometimes called an "endodermis-like layer" or "starch sheath" because of the starch it stores.

### Herbaceous vs woody dicot stems
- **Herbaceous** dicot stems (daisies, tomatoes, basil, all annuals/biennials and most perennials' soft growth) complete their life cycle with little or no secondary growth — the ring of open bundles never becomes a continuous cylinder of wood. They are green, soft, and usually die back to the ground each year.
- **Woody** dicot stems (oak, maple, apple, rose, grape) keep their cambium active year after year, adding xylem (wood) annually and becoming thick, hard trunks (see §4).
- The same plant can have both: an apple tree's *twig* is herbaceous in its first season, woody in its second.

### One-line contrast with monocots (stated once here)
In **monocots** (grasses, lilies, palms, corn), vascular bundles are *scattered* through the stem rather than ringed, each bundle is **closed** (no cambium), so monocots generally cannot thicken by secondary growth the way dicots do.

## 3. Meristems — The Two Growth Engines

A **meristem** is a region of perpetually dividing, undifferentiated cells. Everything else in the plant is its descendant. Primary meristems (from the SAM) differentiate into the **protoderm** (→ epidermis), **ground meristem** (→ cortex and pith), and **procambium** (→ vascular bundles).

- **Apical meristems** — the SAM (shoot tip, §1) and the **root apical meristem (RAM)** (root tip, protected by the **root cap** — a thimble of cells that shields it while it pushes through soil). Apical meristems drive **primary growth**: lengthening.
- **Intercalary meristems** — meristems located *between* mature regions, e.g., at grass nodes and leaf bases. They let a mown lawn regrow from the base. They are essentially a **monocot** feature; **dicots essentially lack intercalary meristems** (their lengthening happens only at the tips).
- **Lateral meristems** — cylinders of dividing cells running the length of the stem, which drive **secondary growth**: thickening. There are two: the **vascular cambium** (→ wood + inner bark, §4) and the **cork cambium / phellogen** (→ bark, §5).

The two growth engines: **primary growth (apical meristems) = length; secondary growth (lateral meristems) = girth.** A tree's height comes from the first, its trunk width from the second.

## 4. Secondary Growth, Step by Step

### Step 1 — The vascular cambium forms
The **vascular cambium** is the thin cylinder of dividing cells that thickens the trunk — literally the "growth ring" of living tissue between wood and bark. It forms when:
- the **fascicular cambium** (the cambium already present *inside* each open bundle) is joined by the **interfascicular cambium** — new meristematic cells that differentiate in the medullary rays *between* the bundles. Together they close the gaps and make one continuous, unbroken ring of cambium around the stem.

### Step 2 — Cambial initials divide
The cambium contains two kinds of **initials** (self-renewing founder cells):
- **Fusiform initials** — long, tapering cells that divide to produce the **axial system**: the lengthwise cells of the wood — vessel elements and tracheids (water-conducting cells), fibers, and axial parenchyma — and the corresponding phloem cells.
- **Ray initials** — small, roughly cubical cells that produce the **radial system**: the **rays** — ribbons of parenchyma running across the growth rings like spokes, storing food and moving it sideways.

### Step 3 — Xylem inward, phloem outward
Each cambial division produces one cell that stays in the cambium and one that differentiates. The daughter cells on the *inside* become **secondary xylem (= wood)**; the daughter cells on the *outside* become **secondary phloem** (part of the inner bark). Because of how the fusiform initials divide unequally, far more cells are added to the xylem side than the phloem side — which is why the wood pile grows to meters while the living phloem stays a few millimeters thick, with older phloem crushed and flattened into a nonconducting layer.

### Step 4 — Annual rings (earlywood/latewood)
In temperate climates the cambium does not grow evenly through the year:
- **Earlywood (springwood)** — produced in spring: wide, thin-walled vessels (water pipes) and pale, low-density tissue. The tree is flushing leaves and needs maximum water flow.
- **Latewood (summerwood)** — produced later in the season: narrower vessels, thicker walls, denser, darker tissue.
One **annual ring** = one earlywood + one latewood band = one year of growth. Count the rings from pith to bark and you have the tree's age — the basis of **dendrochronology** (tree-ring dating), which can also reconstruct past climates from ring widths.

### Step 5 — Sapwood vs heartwood
- **Sapwood** — the outer rings of xylem: still living, still conducting water. Pale in most species.
- **Heartwood** — the inner rings: cells are dead and their contents replaced by **extractives** (tannins, phenols, oils) that color and often protect the wood. Heartwood no longer conducts water; it is purely structural and chemical. As the tree ages, the sapwood/heartwood boundary slowly moves outward.

### Tyloses
**Tyloses** are balloon-like outgrowths of neighboring parenchyma cells that push *into* the hollow vessels of aging xylem and seal them off. They are common in the heartwood of oaks and black locust — the plugged vessels are why oak is water-tight enough for wine barrels and whiskey casks.

### Medullary rays in wood
The **medullary rays** of the primary stem (plus new rays laid down by ray initials) persist in the wood as **rays**: radial sheets of parenchyma crossing the rings. On a quarter-sawn oak board they appear as the pale "ray flecks" that give it its figure.

### Growth rings as age records — and their anomalies
- **Diffuse-porous wood** — vessels of roughly equal size across the whole ring (maple, birch, poplar, beech); ring boundaries are subtle, marked by the denser latewood fiber zone.
- **Ring-porous wood** — one or a few rows of very large earlywood vessels, then small latewood vessels (oak, ash, elm, hickory); ring boundaries are stark and easy to count.
- **Semi-ring-porous** — an intermediate pattern (walnut, black cherry).
- **False rings** — extra, partial rings within one growth year, caused by a mid-season setback (a summer drought or defoliation that stops the cambium, followed by recovery that restarts it). The tree "thinks" the year ended and began again.
- **Missing rings** — years in which severe stress (prolonged drought, fire, heavy defoliation) suppressed the cambium so completely that no xylem ring was laid down at all. Trees on dry, exposed sites commonly have missing rings.
- **Tropical absence** — where the climate is non-seasonal, rings may be faint or absent; in seasonal tropics, rings (if any) track wet/dry alternation rather than temperature.

## 5. Bark

**Bark** (colloquially) is everything outside the vascular cambium. Technically it includes the **periderm** (dead outer layers) and the **phloem** (inner bark). It forms via the second lateral meristem:

- **Cork cambium (phellogen)** — a lateral meristem arising first in the epidermis or outer cortex, later deeper and deeper in the phloem. It divides to produce:
  - **Phellem (cork)** — outward: dead cells whose walls are impregnated with **suberin**, a waxy waterproof substance. Cork is impermeable to water and gas — that's what makes cork stoppers.
  - **Phelloderm** — inward: living parenchyma cells.
- Together, phellogen + phellem + phelloderm = the **periderm** — the replacement "skin" that takes over from the epidermis once secondary growth splits it open.
- **Lenticels** (see §1) are the gas-exchange pores that form within the periderm; they plug the bark with loose, airy cells.
- **Rhytidome** — when successive periderms form deeper and deeper, everything outside the newest one dies and accumulates: that layered dead mass is the rhytidome — the rough outer bark of old oaks and pines.

### Bark types (with examples)
- **Smooth** — beech, hornbeam, young cherry: periderm renews from shallow layers, peeling or stretching without cracking.
- **Scaly / plated** — pine, sycamore (plane), London plane: rhytidome flakes off in irregular plates.
- **Fissured** — oak, ash, elm: deep vertical cracks in a thick rhytidome.
- **Papery / peeling** — paper birch, silver birch: thin sheets of periderm peel horizontally.
- **Corky** — cork oak (the commercial cork source), winged euonymus: enormously thickened, deeply corky phellem.

## 6. Reaction Wood

When a trunk or branch leans (wind, slope, heavy one-sided crown), the tree builds special wood to correct the lean:
- **Tension wood** — in **dicots**, formed on the *upper* side of a leaning stem. Its fibers have a thick, gelatinous, highly cellulosic inner layer that *pulls* the stem back upright. Tension wood is often pale, fuzzy, and causes wood to warp when sawn.
- **Compression wood** — in **gymnosperms** (conifers), formed on the *lower* side, with heavily lignified, dark, brittle tracheids that *push*. (One line, for completeness.)

## 7. Wood Properties

- **Hardwood vs softwood — the botanical meaning**: "Hardwood" = wood of **angiosperm trees** (dicots — oak, maple, walnut); "softwood" = wood of **gymnosperm trees** (conifers — pine, spruce, fir). The names say nothing about actual hardness: balsa (a dicot) is a **hardwood** yet is among the softest, lightest woods on Earth, while yew (a conifer) is a **softwood** harder than many hardwoods. Hardwoods contain **vessels** (hollow pipes); softwoods do not — they conduct only through **tracheids** (long, single-purpose cells).
- **Density** — mass per volume, driven by cell-wall thickness and pore space. Air-dry balsa ~0.16 g/cm³; oak ~0.75 g/cm³; ironwood (hophornbeam / *Ostrya*) among the densest of temperate woods. Fast growth does not automatically mean low density — in ring-porous oaks, *slow*-grown wood is denser; in diffuse-porous woods the relationship flips.
- **Heartwood color** — comes from extractives deposited when sapwood converts to heartwood: black in ebony, deep brown in black walnut, purple in purpleheart, golden-brown in white oak, dark red in cherry and mahogany. Sapwood is pale in nearly all species — a walnut board's pale rim is sapwood.
- **Grain** — the orientation of the axial cells: **straight grain** (spruce), **spiral grain** (sweetgum, many eucalypts), **interlocked grain** (mahogany, teak — gives the ribbon figure of quarter-sawn boards), **wavy grain** (curly maple), and **bird's-eye** (maple — small dormant buds buried under the bark). Rays create the "ray fleck" figure of quarter-sawn oak.
- **How tree age shows in the trunk** — ring count at the base (a 200-year-old oak has ~200 rings at stump height); overall girth; thickening and fissuring of bark; accumulation of heartwood; old scars, branch stubs, and buttressing. The same ring-count method fails only where rings are false, missing, or absent (§4).

## 8. Stem Modifications — The Exhaustive List

A **stem modification** is any stem whose shape and job have been radically reshaped by evolution. The giveaway that a structure is a stem: it has **nodes and internodes**, bears **buds** (not roots), often bears scale leaves, and never has a root cap.

### Underground stems
- **Rhizome** — a horizontal underground stem that grows sideways, storing starch and sending up shoots. Examples: iris, ginger (the edible spice is the rhizome), turmeric, bamboo, ferns, couch grass (the notorious weed spreads by rhizomes). Why: survival through bad seasons, spreading, and storage. It *looks* like a root but has nodes, buds, and scale leaves — a root has none of those.
- **Stolon / runner** — a horizontal *above-ground* stem that roots at its nodes and produces new plantlets. Examples: strawberry runners, spider plant (chlorophytum) babies, mint stolons. Why: clonal spread — each rooted node becomes an independent plant (gardeners call it "layering").
- **Tuber (stem tuber)** — a swollen, fleshy *terminal portion* of an underground stem, built to store starch. The classic is the **potato**: its "eyes" are axillary buds nestled in leaf scars on the tuber's surface, and its "skin" is a periderm — proof it is a stem, not a root. Other stem tubers: Jerusalem artichoke, yam, caladium. *Contrast:* the sweet potato is a **root tuber** (swollen root, no eyes, no nodes) — a different organ doing the same job. Why: starch storage for spring regrowth and winter survival.
- **Corm** — a short, thick, *solid* vertical underground stem, like a squat swollen pillar wrapped in dry scale leaves (a **tunic**). Examples: gladiolus, crocus, freesia, taro. Each year a new corm forms on top of the old one, which shrivels. Why: storage + a compact perennating organ. *Contrast:* a corm is solid stem tissue; a bulb is layered leaves (§ below).
- **Bulb** — a shortened, flattened stem (the **basal plate**) bearing a dense rosette of fleshy, food-storing **modified leaves (scales)**. Examples: onion and tulip (**tunicate bulbs** — the scales are wrapped in a papery tunic of dead leaf bases), lily (**scaly bulbs** — loose, naked scales, no tunic), garlic (each clove is an axillary bud of the bulb). Roots grow from the underside of the basal plate. Why: the most watertight, efficient underground storage + dormancy package — the onion survives months of drought because its scales are sealed leaves. *Note:* a bulb is NOT "a stem" — the stem part is only the basal plate; the bulk is leaf.

### Above-ground modified stems
- **Cladode / phylloclade** — a flattened, green, leaf-like stem that has taken over photosynthesis because the true leaves have been reduced (often to spines, to cut water loss). Examples: the pads of prickly pear cactus (*Opuntia*), butcher's broom (*Ruscus* — its "leaves" are phylloclades bearing flowers in the middle of the blade, which real leaves never do), asparagus fern (*Asparagus setaceus*), leaf cacti (*Epiphyllum*). How to tell it's a stem: flowers, buds, and spines arise on it, and it has no petiole or axil. Why: photosynthesis with minimal water loss in arid habitats.
- **Stem tendril** — a slender, coiling, grasping structure that is a *modified stem* (usually a modified axillary shoot or the abortive end of an inflorescence). Examples: **grape** (tendrils opposite the leaves — a modified shoot), cucumber and other cucurbits, passionflower, Virginia creeper (tendrils tipped with adhesive pads). *Contrast:* the **pea** tendril is a modified *leaf* (leaflet), and **clematis** climbs with modified leaf stalks — same job, different organ. Why: climbing lets a plant reach light without building a thick trunk.
- **Thorn (stem spine)** — a sharp, hard, pointed *modified stem* (a modified branch or axillary shoot). Examples: hawthorn, blackthorn/sloe, honey locust (spectacularly *branched* thorns), **citrus** (the thorns of lemon/orange trees are modified axillary shoots), bougainvillea. Thorns are persistent and firmly attached because they are wood. *Contrast:* a **rose prickle** is NOT a thorn — it is a mere epidermal outgrowth that snaps off; a cactus "spine" is a modified *leaf*. Why: defense against browsing herbivores.
- **Succulent stem** — a thick, fleshy, water-storing stem that also photosynthesizes; the leaves are tiny, spiny, or absent. Examples: the cactus family (saguaro can store tonnes of water in a ribbed, expandable accordion-like trunk), African spurges (*Euphorbia* — cactus lookalikes), *Pachypodium*. Why: the desert survival trick — a big internal water tank with a waxy, spiny armor.
- **Caudex** — a massively swollen stem base, at or just above ground level, used for water and starch storage. Examples: baobab (the "bottle tree" trunk that stores thousands of litres), desert rose (*Adenium*), bottle trees (*Brachychiton*). Why: surviving long droughts and fires — the swollen base is a reserve tank and a source of resprouting buds.
- **Pseudobulb** — the swollen, fleshy aerial stem segment of **sympodial orchids** (Cattleya, Dendrobium, Oncidium). It is a thickened internode that stores water and nutrients and carries leaves; new pseudobulbs arise along a connecting rhizome. Why: epiphytes (plants that grow on other plants, with no soil) need a water tank between rain showers. *Not* a true bulb — it is solid stem tissue with no leaf scales.
- **Stem storage (general)** — beyond these special forms, ordinary stems store: starch in pith/rays (sugar maple), **sucrose in sugarcane** (the whole cane is a stem whose pith is a sugar reservoir), and water in the parenchyma of many succulents.
- **Bonus — bulbils and offsets**: some plants make **bulbils** (tiny detachable storage shoots, e.g., in the inflorescences of some onions and the leaf axils of tiger lilies) and **offsets** (short, crowded side shoots at the plant base, e.g., houseleeks, aloe pups) — both are stem-based clonal propagation.

## 9. Branching

### Apical dominance
**Apical dominance** is the suppression of axillary buds by the terminal bud. The SAM produces the plant hormone **auxin** (indoleacetic acid, IAA), which travels down the stem and inhibits the growth of the axillary buds below it. Consequences: the tree grows as one dominant vertical trunk. Remove the tip (pruning, hedging, browsing, or "pinching" houseplants) and the auxin supply stops, dormant buds burst into branches — which is why hedges thicken and why a topped tree grows a thicket of sprouts. Auxin is also the signal that reactivates the cambium in spring (§11).

### Branching patterns
- **Monopodial branching** — the terminal bud (or SAM) keeps growing year after year, producing a single dominant axis with side branches subordinate to it. Examples: spruce, fir, pine, and young maples — typically trees with a straight central leader.
- **Sympodial branching** — the terminal bud dies, flowers, or aborts each year, and growth continues from a lateral bud that takes over; the trunk is therefore built from a chain of successive shoots, often zigzagging. Examples: horse chestnut, lilac, apple, elm, and many broadleaf trees that combine sympodial twig growth with a broad, rounded crown.
- **Dichotomous branching** — the tip forks into two equal branches, and each fork forks again; rare in dicots (the classic example is mistletoe, *Viscum*; common in some algae and ferns, and in palms — monocots). In most dicots, apparent forks are really sympodial replacements (a "pseudodichotomy").

### Crown architecture
- **Excurrent crown** — a single dominant trunk with side branches, giving a cone or spire shape: spruce, fir, tulip tree. 
- **Decurrent crown** — the trunk quickly subdivides into many co-equal branches, giving a round, spreading head: oak, maple, elm. Crown shape is strongly heritable (oaks are round, poplars are upright columns) and matters for a growth engine's "shape" parameter.

### Branch types
- **Fruit spurs** — short, slow-growing side shoots (often only millimetres per year) that bear the flowers and fruit on apple, pear, cherry, and plum trees; they remain productive for years, which is why orchard pruning preserves them.
- **Epicormic shoots** — shoots that erupt from dormant buds buried under the bark, typically after fire, drought, or heavy damage (oaks, eucalyptus, linden). They are the tree's insurance policy: the trunk can rebuild a crown from scratch.
- **Water sprouts** — vigorous, fast-growing, upright shoots that burst from trunk or branch *surface* buds after over-pruning or stress (apple, maple, linden). They shade the interior and rarely fruit well; arborists remove them.
- **Suckers** — shoots from *root* buds below ground (raspberry, aspen, cherry). Not stems (they arise from roots), but easily confused with water sprouts.

## 10. Tree Trunk Layers — Outside → Inside (the exact stack)

This is the canonical cross-section stack of a mature woody dicot trunk, outermost first. The engine should model these layers in order:

1. **Rhytidome** — the dead outer bark: accumulated old periderms and crushed phloem. Rough, cracked, waterproof. (Absent in young, smooth-barked trunks.)
2. **Periderm (cork layer)** — phellem (dead cork cells, suberized, gas-proof) → phellogen (the cork cambium, the living dividing layer) → phelloderm (living parenchyma). This is the active "skin" that replaces the epidermis.
3. **Phloem (inner bark)** — the living, sugar-conducting tissue: sieve tube elements and companion cells, plus fibers and parenchyma; the outermost part of it is crushed, nonconducting, and becomes part of the rhytidome over time.
4. **Vascular cambium** — the thin (1–10 cells), slick, single ring of dividing cells; the engine of all thickening. Everything inside it is wood; everything outside it is bark.
5. **Sapwood** — the outer, pale, living xylem rings; conducts water; stores starch; is the active "pump" zone.
6. **Heartwood** — the inner, dark, dead xylem; cells plugged with extractives and often tyloses; structural only. (Young trunks may have none.)
7. **Pith** — the central core of primary ground tissue; usually crushed or hollow in an old trunk, but always the innermost point — the tree's original baby stem.

*Simplified primary-stem version (young twig):* epidermis → cortex → phloem → cambium → xylem → pith. As the trunk ages, the cortex and epidermis are shed and replaced by periderm; the pith is left at the very center, where it was on day one.

## 11. Growth Patterns

### Indeterminate vs determinate growth
- **Indeterminate growth** — the SAM keeps producing new nodes for the whole life of the plant; most trees and vines grow this way, which is why a maple can be 200 years old and still elongating. Indeterminate also describes tomato varieties that keep flowering and fruiting until frost.
- **Determinate growth** — the stem (or shoot) stops elongating after a fixed number of nodes or after flowering. Many inflorescences (flower stalks) are determinate; "determinate" tomato varieties grow to a fixed size and stop; rosette plants flower once and die. A tree's *shoots* may be determinate (preformed in the bud, one flush per year — e.g., oak, beech) even while the tree as a whole is indeterminate.

### Seasonal growth cycles (temperate dicots)
- **Dormancy** — in autumn, shortening days and falling temperatures push the tree into dormancy: leaves senesce and fall, buds seal up with scales, and the **cambium stops dividing** for the winter. Dormant buds require a cold period (chilling) before they can grow again — a built-in calendar that prevents spring growth during a January thaw.
- **Spring reactivation** — lengthening days and warming soil trigger bud break; the expanding buds pour **auxin** down the stem, and auxin is precisely the signal that wakes the vascular cambium. The cambium divides, laying down **earlywood** (wide vessels, for the water demand of the new leaf flush). As summer proceeds and water becomes scarcer, the cambium switches to **latewood** (narrow, thick-walled cells). In autumn it stops — completing the annual ring.
- **The growth seasons in one line**: cambium active in spring and early summer, dormant in winter; ring = one active season; and the same hormonal machinery (auxin from buds) couples shoot elongation and trunk thickening — which is why a year with a great crown is usually a year with a wide ring.
