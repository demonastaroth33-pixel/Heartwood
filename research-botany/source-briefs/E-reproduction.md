# Dicot Reproduction — Complete Research Brief

**Scope:** Flowers, inflorescences (complete taxonomy), pollination, fruits (complete taxonomy), seeds, dispersal, tree reproductive timeline. For a "Life Tree" growth engine master reference. Dicot-focused; monocot examples flagged. Every term defined inline.

## 1. The Flower — Anatomy & Architecture

A **flower** = the reproductive shoot of an angiosperm: a compressed stem — the **receptacle** (tip of the flower stalk where all parts attach) — bearing modified leaves (sepals, petals) and sex organs (stamens, carpels). Its stalk is the **pedicel** (or **peduncle** if it holds an inflorescence). Flowers are **solitary** or grouped into **inflorescences** (§2).

### 1.1 The whorls (concentric rings, outside → inside)

- **Calyx (sepals):** outermost whorl of green leaf-like **sepals**; protects the bud; often persistent (tomato).
- **Corolla (petals):** showy whorl of **petals**; color + scent attract pollinators. **Fused** (sympetalous trumpet, morning glory) or **free** (choripetalous, rose).
- **Perianth / tepals:** when sepals and petals look alike, each organ is a **tepal** and together they form the **perianth** (tulip, magnolia).
- **Androecium (stamens):** male whorl. Each **stamen** = **filament** (stalk) + **anther** (pollen sac). **Pollen** = the male gametophyte grain carrying sperm cells.
- **Gynoecium (carpels/pistil):** female whorl. A **carpel** is the ovule-bearing unit (a folded, chamber-like leaf). Fused carpels form the **pistil**: **stigma** (sticky pollen-catching tip), **style** (stalk), **ovary** (base holding **ovules** = immature seeds, attached to a **placenta** inside the **locule**). **Simple** pistil = one carpel (pea); **compound** = fused (tomato). Many free carpels = **apocarpous** (buttercup, magnolia); fused = **syncarpous**.

### 1.2 Completeness & sex

- **Complete:** all four whorls. **Incomplete:** any missing (wind-pollinated flowers often lack petals).
- **Perfect (bisexual):** both stamens and pistil. **Imperfect (unisexual):** one sex — **staminate** or **pistillate**.
- **Monoecious** ("one house"): male AND female flowers on one plant (oak, corn, squash, birch). **Dioecious** ("two houses"): sexes on SEPARATE plants (willow, holly, asparagus, fig). **Polygamous:** mixed.

### 1.3 Floral symmetry

- **Actinomorphic (radial):** divisible equally in many planes — wheel-like (rose, tomato); ancestral, commonest.
- **Zygomorphic (bilateral):** mirror halves in one plane only — face-like (pea, snapdragon, orchid, mint); tied to specialist pollinators.
- **Asymmetric:** no symmetry plane (canna).

### 1.4 Ovary position

- **Hypogynous / superior:** sepals/petals/stamens attach BELOW the ovary (buttercup, tomato, pea).
- **Perigynous / half-inferior:** parts attach around the ovary on a cup-like **hypanthium** (floral tube of fused sepal/petal/stamen bases); ovary free inside (cherry, rose).
- **Epigynous / inferior:** ovary sunken below the attachment; parts appear to arise from its top (apple, cucumber, sunflower). Inferior ovaries yield many **accessory fruits** (§4.6).

### 1.5 Floral formula

Shorthand: **K** (calyx), **C** (corolla), **A** (androecium), **G** (gynoecium), with counts; parentheses = fused; bar over G = inferior. Tomato: `K(5) C(5) A5 G(2)`. The floral **diagram** is the same drawn as a cross-section.

### 1.6 Development from meristems

The **floral meristem** = shoot-tip cells that switch from leaves to flower organs. **ABC model** (one line): gene classes A, B, C expressed in concentric zones set organ identity — A = sepals, A+B = petals, B+C = stamens, C = carpels; mutants swap organs.

### 1.7 Why flowers are small or clustered

Small flowers are cheap but invisible; clustering into **inflorescences** gives a big display for less tissue, spreads risk (frost kills some flowers, not all), and enables "assembly-line" pollination (one visitor services many flowers). Large single flowers (magnolia) are the ancestral beetle-pollination strategy.

## 2. Inflorescences — The Complete Taxonomy (CHECKLIST)

An **inflorescence** = a cluster of flowers on a shared branch system. Terms: **peduncle** = main stalk; **rachis** = flower-bearing axis; **pedicel** = a flower's stalk; **bract** = reduced leaf subtending an inflorescence/flower (bracts ringing a head = **involucre**); **floret** = one small flower of a tight cluster. Key split: **indeterminate (racemose)** — axis keeps growing, oldest flowers at bottom/outside, youngest at tip — vs **determinate (cymose)** — terminal flower forms first (oldest at tip/center), growth stops. *Verified against Britannica, Wikipedia, Ohio Plants, ibiblio botany glossary.*

### 2.1 RACEMOSE (indeterminate)

- **Raceme** — unbranched axis, stalked (pedicellate) flowers, oldest at base. *Ex:* mustard, radish (Brassicaceae), foxglove, lupine.
- **Spike** — raceme with **sessile** (stalkless) flowers. *Ex:* amaranth (Amaranthaceae — dicot), wheat (Poaceae — monocot), plantain.
- **Spikelet** — a tiny spike; the basic unit of grass/sedge inflorescences (Poaceae, Cyperaceae — nearly all monocots; no common dicot spikelet). Grass "heads"/"panicles" are arrangements of spikelets.
- **Catkin (ament)** — soft, drooping, usually **unisexual** spike/raceme of tiny flowers, typically wind-pollinated, falling as a unit. *Ex:* willow (Salicaceae), birch (Betulaceae), oak (Fagaceae), walnut, poplar — pussy willows, dangling oak "tails".
- **Spadix + spathe** — spike of tiny flowers embedded in a thick fleshy axis (**spadix**) wrapped by a showy hooded bract (**spathe**). *Ex:* arum family (Araceae — monocots): jack-in-the-pulpit, peace lily, anthurium, Amorphophallus; some dicots too (Piper).
- **Corymb** — flat-topped raceme: axis elongated but lower pedicels longer, so all flowers level out. *Ex:* candytuft (Iberis, Brassicaceae), cherry (Rosaceae), hawthorn. (A flat-topped cyme = **cymose corymb**.)
- **Umbel** — short axis, equal pedicels from one point, umbrella-like. *Ex:* onion (Allium — monocot), ivy (Araliaceae — dicot), milkweed (Apocynaceae). Classic family: Apiaceae ("Umbelliferae").
- **Capitulum (head; technically calathid)** — very condensed raceme: tiny **sessile** flowers on an expanded receptacle (**torus**) ringed by an involucre. Signature family: Asteraceae (sunflower, daisy, thistle, dandelion); also teasel (Dipsacaceae). See ray/disk (§2.3).
- **Panicle** — branched (compound) raceme: repeated branching, pyramidal, branches ending in flowers. *Ex:* oat (Poaceae — monocot), lilac (Oleaceae), buckeye, chestnut. Strictly = branched indeterminate inflorescence (loose usage: any branched cluster).

### 2.2 CYMOSE (determinate — oldest at tip/center)

- **Cyme (dichasial)** — central flower opens first, younger flowers on branches below; typically flat-topped. Classic **dichasium** (two lateral branches per node, oldest flower in the fork): chickweed, carnation (Caryophyllaceae); strawberry-bush (Celastraceae).
- **Monochasium** — one lateral branch per node, forming a coiled/zigzag chain:
  - **Helicoid cyme (bostryx)** — coils to one side (forget-me-not — Boraginaceae; daylily).
  - **Scorpioid cyme (rhipidium)** — branches alternate sides, zigzag (comfrey, viper's bugloss — Boraginaceae; some nightshades).
  - **Cincinnus** — tight helicoid cyme with very short stalks (dayflower).
- **Polychasium (pleiochasium)** — >2 lateral branches per node (euphorbia's wheel of cyathia, elder/Sambucus).
- **Umbelliform cyme** — compressed cyme mimicking an umbel (common in Allium; true umbels are racemose).

### 2.3 COMPOUND & SPECIAL

- **Compound raceme (double raceme)** — racemes on a raceme; the general form of the **panicle** (§2.1).
- **Compound umbel** — umbels on umbels: primary rays bear secondary umbels (**umbellets**), each with a tiny involucre. Signature family: Apiaceae — carrot, dill, parsley, fennel, poison hemlock.
- **Compound corymb** — corymb of corymbs (branched, flat-topped). *Ex:* hawthorn (Rosaceae), yarrow (Achillea — Asteraceae, corymbs of capitula).
- **Compound spike** — spike of spikes (wheat = spike of spikelets).
- **Compound capitulum** — heads of heads (buttonbush, Cephalanthus — Rubiaceae; some Asteraceae).
- **Thyrse / thyrsus** — indeterminate (racemose) main axis bearing **cymose** side branches (usually dichasia); compact, often pyramidal. *Ex:* lilac (Oleaceae), grape (Vitaceae), horse chestnut. Often mislabeled "panicle."
- **Verticillaster** — whorls of small dichasial cymes (each reduced to a scorpioid cyme) in opposite leaf axils, forming false rings around the stem. Signature family: Lamiaceae — deadnettle (Lamium), mint, sage, basil. Stacked verticillasters look like a spike.
- **Cyathium** — a **pseudanthium** (below): cup-like involucre holding ONE tiny female flower (stalked 3-carpel ovary) ringed by male flowers reduced to single stamens, plus nectar glands. Looks like one flower, is many. Signature genus: Euphorbia (Euphorbiaceae) — poinsettia's "petals" are glands/bracts; cyathia arranged in umbels or thyrsoids.
- **Hypanthodium / syconium** — fig inflorescence: hundreds of tiny unisexual flowers lining the inside of an inverted hollow urn-shaped receptacle (the **syconium**), opening at the top (**ostiole**). *Ex:* fig (Ficus, Moraceae); pollinated by fig wasps entering the ostiole. The mature syconium is the fruit we eat (§4.5).
- **Fascicle** — tight flower cluster from one axil (dogwoods).
- **Glomerule** — dense indeterminate cluster of sessile flowers (some catchflies, Caryophyllaceae).
- **Ray vs disk florets (capitulum in detail)** — in Asteraceae heads: **disk florets** = inner, tubular, usually bisexual; **ray florets** = outer, flattened, strap-shaped (**ligulate**), often female/sterile — the "petals" of a daisy/sunflower. Heads can be all-ray (dandelion), all-disk (thistle), or both (sunflower).
- **Pseudanthium (the big lesson)** — a flower-LIKE structure made of MANY flowers: cyathium, syconium, most capitula. "One flower" can be many flowers — a sunflower head is a population of florets evolved to look like one bloom to recruit pollinators. Same trick: hydrangea's "petals" are sterile florets; dogwood's are bracts.

## 3. Pollination & Fertilization

**Pollination** = pollen transfer from anther to stigma. **Self-pollination** (same flower, or **geitonogamy** = between flowers of one plant) preserves adapted genotypes but inbreeds; **cross-pollination** (**xenogamy**) mixes genes and is favored by most showy flowers. Selfing is avoided via:
- **Cleistogamy** — flowers that never open, selfing in the bud (violets).
- **Dichogamy** — male/female parts mature at different times (**protandry** = stamens first; **protogyny** = stigma first; plantain, walnut).
- **Herkogamy** — physical separation of anthers and stigma (orchids, mints).
- **Self-incompatibility** — the plant biochemically rejects its own pollen (brassicas, cherries, apples — why orchards need two varieties).

### 3.1 Pollination syndromes

- **Entomophily (insects)** — commonest. **Bees:** blue/yellow flowers, UV **nectar guides** (patterns invisible to us pointing at nectar), mild scent, landing platforms (snapdragon, salvia, foxglove); "buzz pollination" — bees vibrate **poricidal** (pore-opening) anthers to shake out pollen (tomato, blueberry). **Butterflies:** red/orange, long tubular nectar spurs (phlox, milkweed). **Moths:** white/pale, strong evening scent, deep tubes (evening primrose, jasmine). **Flies:** dull red-brown, carrion smell, flesh-like texture (jack-in-the-pulpit, many umbels). **Beetles:** primitive syndrome — large open flowers, heavy pollen (magnolia, water lilies).
- **Anemophily (wind)** — no/reduced perianth, no scent, huge amounts of light smooth pollen, exposed feathery stigmas, flowering before leaves unfurl. Signature structure: **catkins** (oak, birch, walnut, cottonwood, grasses, ragweed).
- **Hydrophily (water)** — aquatic; pollen or whole male flowers drift to submerged stigmas (seagrasses, Vallisneria, pondweeds).
- **Ornithophily (birds)** — hummingbirds/sunbirds: bright red/orange, tubular, scentless, copious dilute nectar, tough tissue (trumpet creeper, columbine, salvia). Birds see red but lack smell.
- **Chiropterophily (bats)** — night-blooming, large pale flowers, strong musty/fruity scent, much nectar (baobab, agave, durian).
- **Coevolution examples:** (1) Yucca + yucca moth — the moth pollinates deliberately and lays eggs in the ovary; larvae eat some seeds, the rest get carried (obligate mutualism). (2) Fig + fig wasp — wasps breed inside the syconium and pollinate en route; one wasp species per fig. (3) Ophrys orchids — "pseudocopulation": the lip mimics a female bee; males attempt mating and transfer pollen. (4) Hawkmoths + long-tubed trumpet flowers no bee can reach.

### 3.2 From pollen to seed

1. Pollen lands on the **stigma**; compatible grains germinate.
2. A **pollen tube** grows down the style to the ovary, chemically guided.
3. **Double fertilization** (unique to angiosperms): two sperm travel the tube — one fuses with the egg → **zygote** (→ **embryo**); the second fuses with the central cell → **endosperm** (triploid in most plants: nutritive tissue for embryo or seedling).
4. The ovule matures into the **seed**; the ovary wall matures into the **fruit** (§4). No fertilization, no seed; some plants set fruit without seeds (**parthenocarpy** — seedless banana, navel orange).

## 4. Fruits — The Complete Taxonomy (CHECKLIST)

A **fruit** = the mature ovary (plus sometimes other flower parts) containing seeds. The fruit wall is the **pericarp**: **exocarp** (skin), **mesocarp** (flesh), **endocarp** (inner layer) — sometimes fused beyond recognition. Grouped by origin — **simple** (one ovary), **aggregate** (many ovaries in ONE flower), **multiple** (ovaries of MANY flowers) — and texture/opening: **dehiscent** = splits to release seeds; **indehiscent** = stays closed, disperses whole. *Verified against Wikipedia, Biology LibreTexts, Digital Atlas of Ancient Life, UMN "Science of Plants."*

### 4.1 SIMPLE FLESHY

- **Berry** — whole pericarp fleshy; seeds embedded in pulp, no stone. *Ex:* tomato, grape, blueberry, pepper, banana ("leathery berry"), currant, gooseberry, cranberry. (Gourds and citrus are specialized berries — below.)
- **Drupe (stone fruit)** — thin skin, fleshy mesocarp, HARD stony endocarp ("pit") around one seed. *Ex:* peach, cherry, plum, apricot, olive, mango, almond (the "nut" is the pit), coconut (a **fibrous drupe**). Walnuts/pecans are drupe-like too.
- **Pome** — fleshy fruit whose edible bulk is mostly the **hypanthium** (floral cup) fused around the ovary; the papery "core" is the true pericarp. *Ex:* apple, pear, quince (Rosaceae); rose hip. A pome is an **accessory fruit** (§4.6).
- **Hesperidium** — berry with a leathery rind (peel with oil glands) and juice sacs inside (modified hairs/trichomes filling the locules). *Ex:* orange, lemon, grapefruit — all citrus (Rutaceae).
- **Pepo** — berry with a hard rind, fleshy interior, from an **inferior ovary**, no internal partitions. *Ex:* cucumber, melon, watermelon, squash, pumpkin — gourd family (Cucurbitaceae).

### 4.2 SIMPLE DRY, DEHISCENT

- **Follicle** — one carpel, splits along ONE seam; often plumed seeds. *Ex:* milkweed pods, columbine, larkspur; magnolia and peony fruits are aggregates of follicles.
- **Legume (pod)** — one carpel, splits along TWO seams; signature fruit of Fabaceae. *Ex:* pea, bean, peanut (the shell is the pod; peanuts are seeds), clover, locust trees.
- **Silique / silicle** — two-carpel fruit with a false partition (**replum**); the outer walls (valves) fall away, leaving the seed-bearing frame. **Silique** = elongated (mustard, radish); **silicle** = short/round (shepherd's purse) — both Brassicaceae.
- **Capsule** — dehiscent fruit of TWO OR MORE fused carpels, opening several ways:
  - **Poricidal** — pores (poppy — the pepper-pot lid; bellflower).
  - **Loculicidal** — down the middle of each carpel's chamber (cotton, iris, lily).
  - **Septicidal** — along the partitions between carpels (foxglove, some Dianthus).
  - **Circumscissile (pyxis)** — a lid pops off around the middle (plantain, pimpernel).
  - Also **valvate** (hinged valves: eucalyptus), **denticidal** (teeth at top). Brazil nuts are a capsule, not a nut.

### 4.3 SIMPLE DRY, INDEHISCENT

- **Achene** — one seed, seed coat free from the fruit wall ("shell" separates); often with pappus or hooks. *Ex:* sunflower "seeds" (strictly a **cypsela**, below), buttercup, clematis, sycamore; the "seeds" on a strawberry's surface are each one achene fruit.
- **Cypsela** — achene-like fruit from an INFERIOR ovary, two fused carpels; the correct term for Asteraceae fruits (dandelion, thistle, sunflower). Many books just say achene.
- **Caryopsis (grain)** — one seed FUSED to the pericarp; signature grass fruit. *Ex:* wheat, rice, corn, oats, barley (all Poaceae — monocots).
- **Nut** — one-seeded, hard woody pericarp, often in a cup-like **cupule**/involucre. True nuts: acorn (oak, Fagaceae), hazelnut, chestnut, beech. (Culinary "nuts" — peanut = legume, almond = drupe, cashew = §4.6, coconut = fibrous drupe — are NOT true nuts.)
- **Samara (key fruit)** — winged achene: pericarp grows a flat wing. *Ex:* maple "helicopters" (double samara), ash (single), elm (single), tulip tree (aggregate of samaras).
- **Schizocarp** — does not dehisce but splits into one-seeded **mericarps**. *Ex:* carrot/parsley/dill (Apiaceae — mericarps on a forked stalk), maple (two samara-like mericarps = "samaroid schizocarp"), mallow, linden.
- **Loment** — legume-like fruit breaking into one-seeded segments at constrictions instead of opening. *Ex:* tick trefoil (Desmodium), sweet vetch, sensitive plant (Fabaceae).
- **Utricle** — tiny bladder-like achene with a loose inflated pericarp (beet, Rumex dock, amaranth relatives).
- **Nutlet** — small hard one-seeded indehiscent fruit; the four nutlets at the base of each mint/borage flower (Lamiaceae, Boraginaceae — e.g., basil, forget-me-not).

### 4.4 AGGREGATE fruits (etaerio) — many ovaries, ONE flower

Many free carpels in one flower; each ripens into a fruitlet, all on the same receptacle.

- **Aggregate of achenes** — buttercup, clematis, sycamore; **strawberry** = achenes on a swollen fleshy RECEPTACLE (§4.6 — the red part is not ovary at all!).
- **Aggregate of drupelets** — raspberry (each juicy knob = one little drupe pulling off the white receptacle); blackberry is aggregate-ACCESSORY (receptacle eaten too).
- **Aggregate of follicles** — magnolia (the cone-like "fruit"), peony.
- **Aggregate of berries** — custard apple / soursop / cherimoya (Annona).
- **Aggregate of samaras** — tulip tree; **of capsules** — sweet gum (spiky ball); **of cypselas** — teasel.

### 4.5 MULTIPLE (compound) fruits — ovaries of MANY flowers

The whole **inflorescence** ripens into one fruit; each flower's fruitlet fuses with its neighbors.

- **Syconium** — the inverted hollow fig inflorescence (§2.3) matures into a fleshy "fruit" lined with tiny achenes — fig (Ficus, Moraceae). The fig we eat IS the inflorescence.
- **Sorosis** — fleshy spike of flowers fusing as they ripen: pineapple (Bromeliaceae — the "eyes" are individual flowers; also accessory since bracts/sepals join in), mulberry (Moraceae — each bump is one flower's fruitlet with swollen perianth), osage orange, breadfruit.
- Note: multiple fruits are usually accessory too (axis, bracts, sepals, perianth join the edible mass).

### 4.6 ACCESSORY (false) fruits — true fruit vs swollen extra tissue

An **accessory fruit** (pseudocarp): tissue OTHER than the ovary wall forms a major part. The "false fruit" lesson: what you eat is often not the ovary.

- **Apple/pear (pome):** flesh = hypanthium; true fruit = papery core with seeds.
- **Strawberry:** flesh = swollen receptacle; true fruits = the achenes (each "seed" speck, with a tiny style still attached).
- **Cashew:** the "cashew apple" = hugely swollen PEDICEL (flower stalk); the true fruit is the gray kidney-shaped shell (a drupe-like nut) hanging below — the "cashew nut" is the kernel inside the true fruit.
- **Rose hip:** flesh = hypanthium; true achenes inside.
- **Fig, mulberry, pineapple:** fleshy mass includes inflorescence axis/sepals — multiple-accessory fruits.

## 5. Seeds & Dispersal

A **seed** = a mature ovule: a dormant **embryo** (miniature plant: **radicle** = first root, **hypocotyl**, **cotyledons** = seed leaves, **epicotyl/plumule** = first shoot) wrapped in a **seed coat (testa)** from the ovule's integuments, with stored food (**endosperm** or the cotyledons; **albuminous** seeds keep endosperm — castor bean, coffee; **exalbuminous** absorb it — pea, bean). Landmarks: **hilum** = scar where the seed hung from the ovary wall via the **funiculus** (seed stalk); **micropyle** = pore left by the pollen tube. Distinguish seed (mature ovule) from fruit (mature ovary) — a "grain" is a fruit with one seed.

### 5.1 Dispersal mechanisms

- **Wind (anemochory):**
  - **Samaras/wings** — maple, ash, elm, tulip tree: helicopter spin slows the fall, catching gusts.
  - **Plumes/pappus** — dandelion & thistle (cypselas with parachute pappus), milkweed & cottonwood (plumed seeds from follicles/capsules), willow.
  - **Dust seeds** — orchids: dust-like seeds, thousands per capsule, moved by tiny drafts.
  - **Tumbling** — tumbleweeds break off at the root and roll, scattering seeds (Russian thistle, tumble mustard).
- **Water (hydrochory)** — coconut (fibrous buoyant drupe floating across oceans), water lily seeds, mangrove propagules (**vivipary**: germinating on the parent before dropping).
- **Animals (zoochory):**
  - **Endozoochory (eaten & excreted)** — fleshy fruits (berries, drupes, pomes, figs) eaten; seeds pass through guts and are deposited with fertilizer; some NEED gut passage to germinate (scarification). Birds (mistletoe!), bats, monkeys, bears.
  - **Epizoochory (hitchhikers)** — burrs, hooks, stick-tights catch fur/feathers/clothing: burdock (the model for Velcro!), beggar-ticks (Bidens), cocklebur, stickseed, unicorn plant.
  - **Scatter-hoarding** — jays and squirrels bury acorns/nuts and forget some: jays are oaks' main gardeners.
  - **Myrmecochory (ant dispersal)** — seeds carry a fatty treat (**elaiosome**); ants carry the seed home, eat the elaiosome, dump the seed in their fertilized waste pile: trillium, bloodroot, violets, wild ginger.
- **Mechanical / ballistic (autochory)** — explosive dehiscence flings seeds: touch-me-not / jewelweed (Impatiens — pods explode at a touch), witch hazel (capsules shoot seeds ~10 m), sandbox tree (to 100 m), squirting cucumber, cranesbill geranium, violets.
- **Why dispersal matters** — seeds near the parent compete with it and each other for light, water, nutrients, and are easy targets for seed predators. Dispersal = competition avoidance + colonizing new ground + gene flow between populations.

## 6. The Reproductive Timeline of a Tree

- **Juvenile phase:** young trees make only leaves for years — flowering is suppressed (**juvenility**). Duration varies wildly: birch may flower at 5–10 years; oak typically 20–50 (up to 80 in shade); beech 30–80; some tropical trees flower as 1-year saplings. Juvenility is a SHOOT property, not whole-tree: grafting a mature apple scion onto a juvenile rootstock makes the young tree flower at once (the trick behind dwarf orchards).
- **Flowering cues:** internal signals — **photoperiod** (day length), **vernalization** (winter cold), accumulated spring warmth (**degree days** — the same heat sum that times bud burst), water stress, hormones (gibberellins promote flowering in many). Some species flower by SIZE (minimum canopy volume) rather than age.
- **Mast years:** many forest trees (oak, beech, chestnut, birch) produce near-zero crops most years, then a synchronized **mast year** — a huge acorn/nut boom across whole regions every 2–6 years. Explanations: **predator satiation** (seed-eaters are swamped, some seeds survive) and **resource matching** (masting follows good weather; trees spend stored reserves in a coordinated burst).
- **Flower → fruit timeline (temperate example):** winter buds → spring **bud burst** → **anthesis** (flower opening; oaks flower as leaves emerge, apples after) → pollination (days) → **fruit set** (fertilized flowers only; trees shed excess fruit in the "**June drop**") → summer: cell division then expansion; drupe stones harden (peach pit-hardening) → late summer/autumn **ripening** → seed release. Apple: ~120–180 days bloom to harvest. Oak acorns: flowers in spring, acorns mature the same autumn (white oaks) or the SECOND autumn (red/black oaks).
- **Ripening:** the hormone **ethylene** triggers the **climacteric** — a respiration burst, starch → sugar, softening (cell-wall enzymes), and color change (chlorophyll breakdown reveals carotenoids — orange/red; anthocyanins synthesized — red/blue/purple). Color change is the plant's advertisement: "seed ready, come eat." **Non-climacteric** fruits (citrus, grape, strawberry) ripen only on the plant; **climacteric** (apple, banana, tomato, peach) keep ripening after picking — why a banana in a bag with an apple ripens faster (the apple's ethylene).
- **Abscission & release:** ripe fruits/seeds detach at an **abscission zone** (a weak cell layer at the stalk base); wind, gravity, animals, or dehiscence completes the handoff to §5.
