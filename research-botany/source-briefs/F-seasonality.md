# DICOT Plant Seasonality, Phenology & Life Cycle — Research Brief

## For the "Life Tree" growth engine (master reference)

**What is a dicot?** A flowering plant (angiosperm) whose embryo bears two seed leaves (**cotyledons** — the first leaves packed inside the seed). Dicots have net-veined leaves, flower parts in fours or fives, a **taproot** (one dominant main root), and — in most woody dicots — a **cambium** (a thin cylinder of dividing cells between bark and wood that thickens the trunk every year). Dicots are the vast majority of broadleaf trees (oak, maple, birch, fig, baobab), shrubs, and most herbs. Flowering plants with one seed leaf, parallel-veined leaves, and no true cambium — grasses, lilies, palms, bamboo — are the **monocots**, mentioned below only for contrast. Everything in this brief is about dicots unless stated otherwise.

## 1. Phenology — the plant's internal calendar

**Phenology** (Greek *phainomai*, "to appear") is the study of the timing of recurring life-cycle events — when a plant leafs out, blooms, fruits, colors, and drops its leaves. It is the plant's internal calendar, cued by day length, temperature, and water.

### The seasonal calendar of a temperate dicot
For a Northern Hemisphere temperate tree the year runs: **bud break** (buds open) → **leaf-out** (leaves unfurl and expand) → **flowering** (blossoms open, get pollinated) → **fruiting** (fruits swell, ripen, disperse seeds) → **leaf color** → **leaf fall** (abscission, §7) → winter rest. Timing is species-specific: oaks leaf out *late* (a frost-avoidance strategy — late leaves dodge late frosts); birch and willow leaf out early. Red maple flowers *before* leaves; oaks flower as leaves emerge.

### The standard phenological stages ("Budburst" stages)
The Budburst project (Chicago Botanic Garden) defines observable stages for citizen observers:
- **Bud burst** — buds open, first leaves/flowers visible.
- **First leaf** — the first leaf on a branch is fully unfolded.
- **All leaves unfolded (Full leaf)** — the majority of leaves are fully unfolded.
- **First flower** — first open flower (petals visible).
- **Full flower** — majority of flowers open.
- **First ripe fruit** — first fruit at mature size with ripe seed.
- **Full fruiting** — majority of fruits mature.
- **50% color** — half the leaves show autumn color.
- **50% leaf fall** — half the leaves have dropped.

The scientific standard is the **BBCH scale** — a two-digit code (00–99) covering the whole life cycle: 00–09 germination/seedling emergence, 10–19 leaf development, 20–29 shoot growth, 50–59 inflorescence emergence, 60–69 flowering, 70–79 fruit development, 80–89 ripening, 90–99 senescence (aging, death). Example: BBCH 65 = full flowering; BBCH 92 = leaves falling.

### How timing shifts with place and climate
- **Latitude**: spring arrives later, autumn earlier, at higher latitude (shorter growing season). Day-length change is the reliable cue at high latitudes; temperature dominates at low latitudes.
- **Altitude**: spring events delay roughly 1.5–3 days per 100 m of elevation gain, because air cools ~0.6–1 °C per 100 m (the **lapse rate**).
- **Climate change**: springs arrive earlier (~2–3 days per decade in many temperate regions), autumns end later, and **false springs** (warm spells followed by killing frosts) damage early bloomers like cherry and apple.
- **Coast vs interior**: maritime climates (mild winters, cool summers) delay leaf-out; continental climates (hot summers, hard winters) compress the active season.

Phenology matters ecologically: leaf-out must coincide with pollinator emergence, fruit ripening with seed-disperser migration, leaf fall with winter arrival. A mismatch kills a year's flower and leaf crop.

## 2. Seasonal dormancy

**Dormancy** is arrested growth with reduced metabolism, letting a plant survive a season when growth is impossible (freezing winter, dry season). In woody dicots it is a coordinated shutdown of buds, cambium, and roots.

### Bud dormancy (winter rest)
In autumn, terminal and lateral **buds** (shoot-tips and leaf-axils where next year's leaves and flowers are pre-formed, tiny, inside protective scales) enter rest. They will not grow even in a warm spell — they are waiting for winter.

### The three dormancy types
- **Paradormancy** — suppression by a *neighboring living part*: the **apical bud** (top bud) suppresses lateral (side) buds via the hormone **auxin** (correlative inhibition — this is why a tree is cone-shaped: the tip dominates). Remove the tip and side buds grow.
- **Endodormancy** — dormancy *inside the bud itself*: the bud will not grow even in perfect conditions until it has experienced enough cold. This is the true winter rest.
- **Ecodormancy** — dormancy imposed by *external conditions*: the bud would grow but it is too cold, too hot, or too dry (a mid-winter thaw still below growth threshold).

### Chilling requirement
Most temperate buds must accumulate a **chilling requirement** — a set number of hours below a threshold temperature (commonly 7.2 °C / 45 °F; roughly 0–10 °C) — to break endodormancy. Typical values: apples ~400–1,200 chill hours, peaches ~300–900, blueberries ~800–1,000. Mild-climate species need few (olive, citrus ~0–200). **Climate-change problem**: warm winters no longer deliver enough chill; buds open late, unevenly, or weakly ("delayed foliation"), now seen in peach orchards in the southeastern US.

### Heat requirement (growing degree days)
Chilled buds still need warmth to open — the **heat requirement**, measured in **growing degree days (GDD)**: for each day, (average daily temperature − base temperature), summed over the season. The base is the temperature below which growth stops (commonly 10 °C / 50 °F for temperate trees). Example: a day averaging 18 °C contributes 8 GDD (18 − 10). A late-leafing oak may need 300–400 GDD; an early willow far fewer. **Bud break = enough chill (to wake) + enough GDD (to start).**

### Dormancy release (spring warming)
As days lengthen and temperatures rise, endodormancy → ecodormancy → growth: buds swell, scales shed, pre-formed leaves and flowers expand. The cascade involves water uptake, sugar mobilization, and hormone shifts (gibberellins and cytokinins rise; abscisic acid falls).

### The cambium dormancy/activity cycle
The **cambium** (dividing layer between bark and wood) is the engine of trunk thickening. It is active in spring and summer — dividing outward to make new bark (**phloem**, the food-conducting tissue) and inward to make new wood (**xylem**, the water-conducting tissue) — and dormant in winter. This on/off cycle writes the tree's autobiography: one **annual ring** per growing season. Each ring = **earlywood** (spring: large, thin-walled water pipes for fast flow) + **latewood** (summer: small, thick-walled, dense cells for strength). Count rings and you read the tree's age and each year's climate (wide ring = good year; **dendrochronology**, tree-ring dating, exploits this).

### Root dormancy
Roots are less deeply dormant than buds. Soil buffers air temperature, so in mild winters roots grow slowly whenever soil exceeds ~5–7 °C — the winter **root flush** often precedes bud break. Deep roots are least dormant; **fine roots** (thin, short-lived absorbing roots) die and regrow seasonally.

## 3. Photoperiodism

**Photoperiodism** is a plant's response to the relative length of day and night — its season clock, immune to freak warm days. Plants are classified by flowering response:

- **Short-day plants (SDP)** — flower when nights are LONGER than a threshold (they actually measure the *night*; "long-night plants" is the accurate name). Examples: chrysanthemum (flowers as autumn nights lengthen), poinsettia (needs nights > ~12 h), soybean ('Maryland Mammoth' needs nights > ~13.5 h), rice.
- **Long-day plants (LDP)** — flower when nights are SHORTER than a threshold, as spring days lengthen. Examples: spinach (bolts — shoots up a flower stalk — in long days), lettuce, henbane (*Hyoscyamus niger*), radish.
- **Day-neutral plants** — flower regardless of day length, cued by age or size. Examples: tomato, cucumber, dandelion, many beans.

**Critical day length** — the day/night ratio at which the switch flips. A plant with a 12-hour critical night flowers when nights exceed 12 h (SDP) or fall below it (LDP). The response is graded, not binary.

### Phytochrome — the pigment that measures day length
**Phytochrome** is a blue-green pigment molecule in plant cells with two interconvertible forms: **Pr** (red-absorbing, biologically inactive) and **Pfr** (far-red-absorbing, biologically active). Daylight (rich in red light) converts Pr → Pfr; darkness, or far-red light (the light filtering through a canopy), converts Pfr → Pr. Pfr is the "message" that the day is long: on a long day Pfr accumulates; on a long night it reverts to Pr or is destroyed. The plant reads day length by how much Pfr remains at the critical moment — a long night leaves little Pfr (short-day signal), a short night leaves much (long-day signal). This is the classic "hourglass" model; the refined *external-coincidence* model says a light-sensitive "flowering hormone" (**florigen** — actually the protein FT) accumulates in the dark, and a flash of light mid-night destroys it, resetting the clock.

### When a plant decides to bloom
The decision is made days to weeks before flowers are visible: the **shoot apex** (growing tip) switches from leaves to flower buds once photoperiod (plus temperature, vernalization §4, and internal age) permits. Florigen travels in the phloem from leaves to the apex — a leaf kept in long days can make an entire plant flower, even a grafted partner.

## 4. Vernalization

**Vernalization** (Latin *vernus*, "of spring") is the promotion of flowering by a prolonged period of cold — the plant's proof that winter happened, so it blooms in spring rather than in an autumn warm spell. It is a *qualitative* cold requirement, distinct from bud chilling.

- **Winter annuals** — germinate in autumn, overwinter as small leafy **rosettes** (a flat disc of leaves at ground level), flower early next spring. Examples: winter wheat, winter rye, *Arabidopsis thaliana* (the lab plant).
- **Biennials** — two-year lives: year one is a vegetative rosette storing energy (carrot, beet, cabbage, foxglove, mullein, hollyhock); year two the rosette **bolts** (elongates into a flower stalk) and seeds, after a winter's vernalization. A biennial that misses its cold (an indoor carrot) can stay vegetative for years.
- Some perennials and trees need cold to flower well (many apples need ~1,000 chill hours for uniform bloom; peonies need a cold winter).

**Mechanism (one paragraph):** in *Arabidopsis*, cold progressively silences the gene **FLC**, a flowering repressor — the longer and colder the winter, the more silenced. The silencing is **epigenetic** (a chemical "off switch" on the DNA that persists through cell division), so the plant *remembers* winter all spring. When warm days return, the unblocked flowering program runs. Heat erases the memory, which is why vernalization must be continuous.

## 5. Evergreen vs deciduous vs semi-deciduous strategies

### Leaf lifespan economics — why lose leaves at all?
A leaf is an investment: it costs sugars and nutrients to build and pays back by photosynthesizing. **Deciduous** species (drop all leaves each season — oak, maple, birch, beech) abandon the investment each autumn but dodge winter's fatal problem: leaves **transpire** (lose water vapor), and frozen soil cannot supply water — a leafy tree in January dies of drought. Deciduousness = an annual restart with a fresh, high-efficiency leaf crop.

**Evergreens** (holly, rhododendron, Mediterranean live oaks, most tropical trees) keep leaves 2–7 years, spreading the building cost over many payback seasons. This pays where the growing season is short or the soil nutrient-poor (a new leaf crop each year is expensive when nitrogen is scarce). Their leaves are typically **sclerophyllous** — stiff, leathery, long-lived — with a **thick cuticle** (waxy waterproof skin) and tough fibrous tissue: armor against drought, herbivores, and nutrient loss.

### Drought-deciduous
Where the trigger is water, not cold: **drought-deciduous** plants drop leaves in the dry season to stop transpiration and wait out the drought (baobab, ocotillo, many figs, some acacias). They may leaf out and shed several times a year if rains come in pulses.

### Semi-deciduous
**Semi-deciduous** species drop only part of their leaves (old, shaded, damaged) and keep the rest — some oaks and tropical trees; a tree can be evergreen in a mild year, deciduous in a hard one. Related oddity: **marcescence** — beech and some oaks hold *dead* brown leaves through winter, shedding them at bud break (protection for new buds, or simply a lazy abscission).

### Leaf flush types
- **Flush-type (synchronous) growth** — all new leaves burst at once, usually once or twice a year, often triggered by rain or warmth (mango, many figs, many oaks). New flushes are often red or pink — young leaves rich in protective **anthocyanin** pigments (red-purple) and low in chlorophyll (green) — the "flushing" look of a tropical crown.
- **Continuous growth** — leaves produced steadily through the season (many tropical understory trees, laurels, fast-growing pioneers).

## 6. The four seasons for a temperate dicot tree (month-by-month)

Northern Hemisphere, mid-latitude broadleaf tree (oak or maple):

**Winter (Dec–Feb)** — Bare, fully dormant. Buds closed and chilled; cambium inactive. **Hardiness** (cold tolerance) is maximal: cells accumulate sugars and protective proteins, water moves out of cells into intercellular spaces where ice can form without puncturing membranes, and the tree **supercools** (stays liquid below freezing). A January thaw followed by hard freeze damages this protection; mid-winter frosts are the most lethal. Shallow roots may still grow slowly in unfrozen soil. (Sugar maple mobilizes stored starch into sugar as temperatures rise toward February — the sap run that becomes maple syrup.)

**Spring (Mar–May)** — Dormancy breaks: buds swell and burst (**bud break**), shedding their scales. Early bloomers (red maple, willow, elm, aspen) flower before or with leaf-out, using stored reserves, wind or early bees for pollination. Leaves unfurl and expand (**leaf-out**); the **cambium wakes** and lays down **earlywood** — the wide, thin-walled vessels of the year's ring, plumbing for the coming canopy's water demand. New roots flush. Frost risk is acute: late frosts kill just-opened flowers and tender leaves (the false-spring hazard).

**Summer (Jun–Aug)** — Leaves fully expanded; photosynthesis peaks — the canopy is the tree's solar panel at maximum. Fruits and seeds grow (acorns swell through summer, maple keys ripen). The cambium switches to **latewood** — narrow, thick-walled, dense cells — giving the ring its dark outer band. The tree pre-forms next year's buds (leaf and flower). Height growth slows and stops by late summer; the tree banks sugars and starches in roots and trunk for winter and next spring. Summer drought cuts ring width and stresses the canopy.

**Autumn (Sep–Nov)** — Fruits ripen, seeds disperse (acorns, keys, berries — eaten by jays, squirrels, birds that scatter them). Days shorten, nights cool: **chlorophyll** (the green photosynthetic pigment) breaks down and is salvaged; **carotenoids** (yellow-orange pigments, always present, now unmasked) show — and many trees (maples, oaks, sumac) *synthesize* red-purple **anthocyanins** as a sunscreen protecting nutrient salvage while nitrogen and phosphorus are pulled back into the twigs (**resorption**, up to 50–80% of leaf nitrogen). **Abscission zones** form; leaves fall. The cambium shuts down; the tree **acclimates** (builds hardiness). By November the tree is bare, its mobile nutrients in storage.

**The tropical alternative** — No winter: growth is governed by wet/dry seasons or nothing at all. Evergreen rainforest trees grow year-round or in flushes, flower and fruit on species-specific schedules (many figs fruit several times a year), and their wood often lacks rings or has faint, irregular rings (a dry-season pause can still make one). Seasonally dry tropics (savanna, dry forest) behave like a temperature-less temperate zone: trees go drought-deciduous, drop leaves, regrow with the first rains — a dry-season dormancy homologous to winter rest.

## 7. Abscission — the art of letting go

**Abscission** is the controlled shedding of an organ — leaf, fruit, flower — at a pre-formed **abscission zone**: a band of small, thin-walled cells at the base of the **petiole** (leaf stalk). Two things happen there: an inner **separation layer** whose cells digest their own walls with enzymes (cellulase and polygalacturonase — wall-dissolving proteins), so the leaf separates cleanly; and an outer **protective layer** whose cells become corky (**suberized**) and seal the wound, leaving the **leaf scar** (the flat mark on the twig, dotted with **bundle scars** — the sealed stubs of the leaf's veins).

**The hormonal switch (ethylene + auxin).** **Auxin** (a growth hormone made by the leaf blade) travels to the abscission zone and keeps it shut; **ethylene** (a ripening/senescence gas hormone) promotes separation. As a leaf ages — or day length shortens, cold arrives, or drought strikes — auxin output falls, ethylene rises, the balance flips, the zone digests, the leaf falls. The same machinery drops ripe fruit (apple drop), sheds wilted flowers, and lets a water-stressed tree shed leaves to survive (drought-deciduousness).

**Nutrient resorption** happens *before* the fall: chlorophyll is stripped, nitrogen and phosphorus exported back through the phloem into the twigs and stored as proteins and starch — which is why autumn leaves end up yellow/brown (the pigments left behind) and why forests recycle so efficiently. Fallen leaves then feed the soil food web.

**Timing triggers:** primarily shortening day length (photoperiod) at temperate latitudes, reinforced by low temperature; drought overrides everything in the tropics/deserts; a severe autumn storm can physically strip leaves before the zone is ready.

## 8. Environmental responses — tropisms and nastic movements

A **tropism** is a directional growth response to a directional stimulus — the plant bends *toward* or *away* by growing faster on one side.

- **Phototropism** (light) — stems bend toward light. Light redistributes **auxin** to the shaded side of the stem; that side elongates faster; the tip curves toward the light.
- **Gravitropism** (gravity) — shoots grow up (negative), roots grow down (positive). Gravity is sensed by **statoliths** (starch-filled plastids — small starch-grain packages inside cells) that settle to the lowest side of the cell, signaling which way is down. Auxin redistributes to the lower side: in stems that side grows more (bend up); in roots the lower side is *inhibited* so the upper side grows more (bend down) — opposite responses to the same signal.
- **Thigmotropism** (touch) — **tendrils** (specialized touch-sensitive climbing organs — grape, pea, cucumber, passionflower) coil around supports: touch triggers faster growth on the side away from contact, curling the tendril around the support.
- **Hydrotropism** (water) — roots grow toward moist soil (weaker than gravitropism, but real).
- **Chemotropism** (chemicals) — **pollen tubes** (the tubes a pollen grain grows down the style to reach the ovule/egg) are guided chemically toward the ovule — a sperm-delivery system.
- **Heliotropism** (sun tracking) — some plants rotate organs to follow the sun: young sunflower heads track east→west across the day (stopping when mature), arctic poppies turn their flowers to the sun to warm developing seeds. (Note: the classic "morning glory" behavior is *not* heliotropism — it is nyctinasty, below.)

**Nastic movements** are responses to *non-directional* stimuli — the direction of the response is fixed by the plant's anatomy, not the stimulus direction:
- **Nyctinasty** (sleep movements) — leaves or flowers fold/close at night, open by day: legumes (bean, clover), oxalis (wood sorrel), prayer plant, and morning glory (flowers open at dawn, close by dusk). Driven by **turgor** (internal water pressure) changes in **pulvini** (hinge-like swellings at leaf bases): motor cells pump potassium and water in or out; water-loaded (turgid) cells hold the leaf open, water-lost (flaccid) cells let it fold.
- **Seismonasty** (shock) — the sensitive plant (*Mimosa pudica*) folds its leaflets and droops its whole leaf within a second of being touched: an electrical signal (**action potential**) races down the leaf, motor cells dump water, turgor collapses. It is a defense (startle herbivores, dislodge insects) at the cost of lost photosynthesis while folded.
- **Thermonasty / photonasty** — opening/closing responses to temperature and light levels (crocus and tulip flowers open with warmth).

**Turgor** is the internal water pressure keeping plant cells (and whole herbaceous plants) rigid — the same pressure that drives these movements. Lose turgor and a plant wilts (§9).

## 9. Environmental stress responses

### Water stress (drought)
When soil water runs short: (1) roots send a hormonal alarm — **ABA** (abscisic acid) — to the leaves; (2) **stomata** (leaf-surface pores that let CO₂ in and water out) close, cutting transpiration but also photosynthesis; (3) leaves wilt (turgor loss), curl, or shed (drought-deciduousness, §5); (4) growth stops, roots grow deeper. Chronic adaptations (**xerophytes** — drought specialists): deep taproots (mesquite roots to 50 m are claimed), small or divided leaves (less evaporative surface), thick cuticles and **sunken stomata** (stomata tucked in pits where humid air lingers), leaf hairs that trap moisture, **succulence** (thick water-storing stems/leaves — cacti and stonecrops, both dicots), and **CAM photosynthesis** (cacti, euphorbias, stonecrops open stomata only at night and store CO₂ as acid for daytime use — the most water-efficient carbon fixation known).

### Temperature stress
- **Frost damage** — ice crystals form inside cells and puncture membranes, killing flowers, young leaves, and bark (frost cracks). Plants avoid it via hardiness (§6): sugars act as antifreeze, cells dehydrate, water freezes only between cells.
- **Chilling injury** — tropical plants (tomato, many rainforest species) are damaged even by 0–15 °C: membranes leak, leaves yellow — they lack the protective machinery temperate plants build.
- **Heat stress** — above ~35–40 °C enzymes **denature** (unfold and stop working), photorespiration wastes energy, stomata close (overheating, wilting). Desert plants use thick insulation and light reflection.
- **Sunscald** — sudden exposure of shaded bark (after a neighbor falls, or on the southwest side): sun kills the cambium under the bark, leaving dead cracked patches. Why orchardists paint trunks white.
- **Freeze-thaw embolism** — when xylem sap freezes, dissolved air comes out of solution and bubbles form; on thawing the bubbles block the water columns (**embolism/cavitation** — air-blockage of the water pipes). Ring-porous trees (oak, elm, ash) are especially vulnerable because their wide spring vessels embolize every winter — which is exactly why they must rebuild earlywood each spring.

### Light stress
- **Shade tolerance** — species that survive, photosynthesize, and grow slowly in deep shade (beech, maple, holly) vs **shade-intolerant** species demanding full sun (birch, aspen, willow — the pioneers that die under a closed canopy).
- **Sun leaves vs shade leaves** — dramatic **leaf plasticity** (one genome, many shapes): a sun leaf is smaller, thicker, with more **palisade layers** (dense columnar light-capture cells), more chlorophyll per area, and a higher light-saturation point; a shade leaf is large, thin, with big chloroplasts tuned to dim light. The same tree carries both (sun leaves on the crown top, shade leaves inside).

### Salt stress (halophytes — salt specialists)
Mangroves (dicot trees of tidal coasts — *Rhizophora*, *Avicennia*) and salt-marsh plants cope three ways: **salt exclusion** (roots filter salt out at uptake), **salt excretion** (salt glands pump salt onto leaf surfaces — visible as white crystals), and **succulence** (diluting salt in extra water storage). Salt kills normal plants by osmotic dehydration (roots cannot pull water from salty soil) and ion toxicity (sodium damages enzymes).

### Wind stress
Wind prunes growth on the windward side ("**flag trees**" — branches all streaming downwind), thickens stems, and, when a tree leans, it lays down **reaction wood**: in dicots, **tension wood** — specialized gelatinous-fiber wood on the *upper* side of the lean that contracts and pulls the stem upright. (Conifers do the opposite: compression wood on the lower side, pushing.) Severe storms cause **windthrow** — uprooting or snapping — the fate that ends most old trees.

### Fire adaptation
Thick, corky, insulating bark (cork oak; fire-resprouting eucalypts of Australia); **epicormic resprouting** (dormant buds buried in bark that burst out as new shoots after the crown is scorched — oaks and eucalypts regrow this way); and **serotiny** — seeds held in sealed woody fruits or cones that open only with fire's heat, sowing the next generation into the ash-cleared ground (famous in pines, but also in dicots such as the Australian *Banksia*).

## 10. Plant lifestyles (life-history strategies)

- **Annual** — germinate, grow, flower, seed, die within one year (or growing season). Nearly all temperate annuals are **monocarpic** (below) and overwinter *as seeds*. Examples: poppy, cornflower, most garden weeds, desert ephemerals (which race from seed to seed in the few weeks after rain).
- **Biennial** — two years: vegetative rosette the first, flower and die the second (§4). Examples: carrot, parsnip, foxglove, mullein, hollyhock.
- **Perennial** — live many years. **Herbaceous perennials** (peony, aster, wild ginger) die back to the ground each winter, surviving as **roots, bulbs, corms, or rhizomes** (underground storage organs) that regrow shoots each spring. **Woody perennials** — trees and shrubs — keep their above-ground frame and enter dormancy (§2).
- **Monocarpic** ("once-fruiting") — bloom once, then die; the whole plant's energy goes into one massive flowering. Classic examples are monocots (the century plant/agave — so named because it may live decades before flowering once; bamboos, which flower simultaneously every ~60–120 years), but dicots include the giant lobelias of African mountains and most biennials. **Semelparous** is the synonym ("one reproductive event").
- **Polycarpic** ("many-fruiting") — flower and fruit many times over a long life: most trees and shrubs (oak, maple, apple). **Iteroparous** is the synonym. Trees often **mast** — flower heavily some years, lightly in others (acorn masting cycles of 2–5 years; mast years saturate seed-eaters, leaving survivors).

**Overwintering strategies, by lifestyle:** annuals = dormant seeds (the seed coat is their winter armor); herbaceous perennials = underground storage organs; woody perennials = dormant buds + dormant cambium + cold-hardened tissues. In warm climates the "winter" is a dry season survived by drought-deciduousness or dormancy.

## 11. The tree life cycle — seed to death

1. **Seed** — the embryo plus food supply in a coat. Temperate tree seeds are dormant and may need **stratification** (a cold, moist period — the seed's own chilling requirement) or **scarification** (abrasion of the coat, often by passing through an animal gut or fire) before germinating.
2. **Germination** — the seed **imbibes** (takes up water), swells, and the **radicle** (embryonic root) emerges first, anchoring and drinking; then the shoot emerges — **epigeal** if the seed leaves (cotyledons) rise above ground (maple, bean), **hypogeal** if they stay below (oak, walnut: the acorn stays buried, the shoot rises alone).
3. **Seedling** — the first years: high mortality (shade, drought, herbivores, trampling). Survival depends on shade tolerance (§9) and stored reserves. Establishment = taproot + first true leaves.
4. **Sapling** — a young tree with strong **apical dominance** (top bud suppresses side branches — one straight leading shoot). Fast growth; high mortality as the canopy closes; suppressed saplings may linger for decades in shade, then burst when a gap opens (the **seedling bank** strategy of shade-tolerant species).
5. **Pole stage** — the trunk is tall and straight; lower branches die and drop (**self-pruning**); the tree races upward toward the canopy — the dense young-stand "thicket" stage.
6. **Mature stage** — the **crown** (leafy top) reaches full canopy size; flowering and fruiting begin in earnest (trees often flower years before, but production peaks now); height growth slows toward a **height plateau** (a species-typical maximum set by genetics and by water-transport physics), while the trunk keeps thickening (**secondary growth** — the cambium lays down annual rings for life).
7. **Old-growth / senescent stage** — growth slows further; the crown begins **dieback** (outer and top branches die, giving the ragged "stag-headed" silhouette); the canopy thins; the **heartwood** (dead, structural core) may hollow as **heart rot** (wood-decay fungi) consumes it — hollow old trees (baobabs, oaks) stand and stay vigorous for centuries, the shell doing the work.
8. **Death** — usually not old age alone but accumulated weakness: windthrow (§9), root rot, drought, lightning, insects (bark beetles), disease. Dead standing trees ("**snags**") are ecological gold — homes for birds, fungi, insects.

**Forest succession roles.** **Pioneer species** (birch, aspen, poplar, black cherry, alder) colonize open ground first: they demand full sun (shade-intolerant), grow fast, make many wind- or bird-dispersed seeds, are short-lived (60–120 years), and die in shade. Under their thin canopy, **climax species** (beech, sugar maple, hemlock; oaks are intermediate) establish: shade-tolerant, slow-growing, long-lived, able to replace themselves in their own shade. Classic sequence: field → pioneer thicket → mixed forest → late-successional forest; each generation creates the shade and soil the next prefers. Succession is not destiny — disturbance resets it, climate shifts it.

**Lifespan extremes.** The oldest individual trees are *conifers* (gymnosperms — not dicots): bristlecone pines ~4,850 years (Methuselah; a neighbor may exceed 5,000), giant sequoias ~3,200. The oldest *dicots*: the sacred fig (*Ficus religiosa*) of Sri Lanka — a fig, planted by humans, with ~2,300 years of recorded history — is the best-documented dicot; radiocarbon-dated African baobabs run ~1,275 years (confirmed) with claims to ~2,100 (older multi-thousand-year claims were discredited); olive trees claim 2,000+ (uncertain); veteran oaks reach 800–1,200 (the Bowthorpe Oak ~1,000). Contrast the pioneers: birch, aspen, and poplar rarely exceed 100–150 years; box elder and cherry ~100. Lifespan correlates with strategy: slow, dense, shade-tolerant, stress-resistant = long; fast, light-wooded, sun-loving = short. The same tradeoff shapes ring patterns (§12).

## 12. Growth patterns through life

- **The growth curve is sigmoid** (S-shaped): slow at first (a seedling's small leaf area), accelerating to a rapid middle phase (the sapling/pole growth spurt — compounding leaf area), then flattening to a plateau as the tree nears its height limit and canopy self-shading caps net photosynthesis. Height follows this curve most strictly; **basal area** (trunk cross-section) and **biomass** keep increasing longer, because the cambium keeps adding a ring of wood every year regardless of height.
- **Ring width vs age** — young trees lay down wide rings (small circumference, big canopy, fast growth); old trees lay down narrow rings (huge circumference, declining vigor, self-shading) — so ring width declines with age even in a good year. Foresters read this as **current annual increment** (this year's wood) vs **mean annual increment** (lifetime average); the two cross when growth peaks, and harvest decisions hang on that crossing.
- **Juvenile vs adult wood** — the inner core of rings (**juvenile wood**) is lower density, with thinner cell walls and shorter, weaker cells, laid down while the crown was young and fast; the outer rings (**mature wood**) are denser and stronger. Hence the same species' wood quality improves toward the bark — and why old-growth timber is prized.
- **Crown spread vs height** — height plateaus early (biology caps it), but the crown keeps spreading for decades; an old open-grown oak's canopy diameter can exceed its height. Crown form depends on crowding: a forest-grown tree is tall with a small, high crown (competition prunes the lower branches); an open-grown tree is short and wide-crowned. Crown dieback at senescence reverses even this — the top loses branches while the lower crown widens.
